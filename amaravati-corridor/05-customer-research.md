# Phase 5 — Customer Research
## The realistic buyer universe, tested before any audience is assigned

**Prepared:** August 2026
**Purpose:** Audience foundation for the hypothetical 25-acre, ₹500 crore corridor development
**Sequencing note:** The brief is explicit — *do not decide the audience for Communities A/B/C/D before analysing the market.* This document analyses the universe first (§1–§4), prioritises (§5), and only then derives a community mapping (§6) as an output rather than an input.

---

## 0. Method, and an honest statement about what this is

### 0.1 What kind of customer research this is — and is not

**No primary buyer research was possible.** No surveys, no interviews, no focus groups, no CRM data, no walk-in analysis. Phase 2 §15 flagged this as evidence gap #3 and it remains the largest gap in the assignment.

**What this document is instead:** a **reconstruction of the buyer universe from three things that can be established** —

1. **The financial gate.** Who can arithmetically afford each ticket band, computed from published lending rules.
2. **The income sources that exist in this catchment.** Published salary scales, district income data, trade-sector economics, diaspora numbers, and the land-pooling settlement.
3. **National buyer-behaviour research**, applied with a stated caution about its transferability.

**This is a defensible way to size and rank segments. It is not a substitute for talking to buyers.** Every behavioural statement below — motivations, objections, timelines, channels — is labelled by how it was arrived at, and §7 lists what primary research would need to test.

### 0.2 The transferability caution on national research

The best available buyer survey is **Knight Frank's *Beyond Bricks: The Pulse of Home Buying* (2025)** — 1,629 homebuyers. It covers **Ahmedabad, Bengaluru, Chennai, Hyderabad, Kolkata, MMR, NCR and Pune.** It does **not** cover Vijayawada, Guntur or Amaravati.

Magicbricks' 2026 buyer research and the various "new-age homebuyer" studies have the same limitation: metro panels, applied to a Tier-2 corridor.

> **Applied rule throughout:** national findings are used for **direction** (what people weigh, roughly in what order) and never for **magnitude** in this catchment. Where a national figure would mislead if taken literally, that is stated.

### 0.3 The three labels used

| Label | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Established** | Computed from published rules, or drawn from a named data source about this catchment |
| **Applied** | A national or sector finding transferred to this market, with the transfer risk stated |
| **Reasoned** | My inference from market structure. Testable, and flagged for testing |

## 1. The financial gate — who can arithmetically buy what

Before asking who *wants* each product, it is worth establishing who *can pay for it*. This filters the universe hard, and it is computable.

### 1.1 The lending rules, as published

| Rule | Value | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Loan-to-value, above ₹75 lakh | **75%** | RBI guideline |
| Loan-to-value, ₹30–75 lakh | 80% | RBI guideline |
| FOIR — total EMIs as share of gross monthly income | **40–55%** | Lender norm |
| Home loan rate, this region | **7.15–7.35%** | Phase 1 §6.2 |
| Transaction cost (stamp + registration + transfer) | **7.5%** | Phase 1 §6.2 |
| Co-applicant effect on eligibility | **+50–80%** | Lender norm |
| Share of urban Indian buyers using a home loan | **79%** | Knight Frank, 2025 |

### 1.2 What each community actually requires

Computed at a 20-year tenure and 7.25%, using the midpoint of each Phase 2 ticket band.

| | **Community A** | **Community B** | **Community C** | **Community D** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ticket band (Phase 2 §12) | ₹58–70 L | ₹89 L – 1.05 Cr | ₹1.44 – 1.75 Cr | ₹2.00 – 2.57 Cr |
| Midpoint used | ₹64 L | ₹97 L | ₹1.60 Cr | ₹2.28 Cr |
| Max loan at LTV | ₹51.2 L (80%) | ₹72.8 L (75%) | ₹1.20 Cr (75%) | ₹1.71 Cr (75%) |
| Monthly EMI | **₹40,500** | **₹57,500** | **₹94,800** | **₹1,35,200** |
| **Gross household income needed** *(FOIR 40–45%)* | **₹11 – 14 lakh p.a.** | **₹15 – 19 lakh p.a.** | **₹25 – 30 lakh p.a.** | **₹36 – 43 lakh p.a.** |
| Down payment | ₹12.8 L | ₹24.3 L | ₹40.0 L | ₹57.0 L |
| Stamp duty etc. at 7.5% | ₹4.8 L | ₹7.3 L | ₹12.0 L | ₹17.1 L |
| **Cash needed at purchase** | **~₹17.6 L** | **~₹31.5 L** | **~₹52.0 L** | **~₹74.1 L** |

### 1.3 Two consequences that shape everything after this

**First — the cash barrier is the real gate, not the income test.** A Community D buyer needs **₹74 lakh in hand** before a single EMI. In a market where Phase 2 established that trading and agricultural wealth dominates, **cash availability discriminates more sharply than documented income does** — and it favours business families and asset-sellers over salaried professionals, even where the salaried buyer technically qualifies.

**Second — the income required for Community D is roughly 10× the district's per capita GDP.** NTR district (Vijayawada) records per capita GDP of **₹3,60,354** and Guntur **₹3,04,175** (2023-24); the AP average is ₹2,66,240, itself 1.30× the national figure. Per capita GDP is not household income, but the ratio is directional: **a ₹36–43 lakh household income is a very thin slice of this catchment.**

> **Established.** Community D cannot be sold primarily to salaried locals. It requires business income, diaspora income, professional income at the top of institutional scales, or asset conversion. That is a finding about the *universe*, arrived at before any segment was chosen.

## 2. The income sources that actually exist in this catchment

Segments are only real if the money behind them is real. This section establishes what the catchment's income sources are and what each can afford.

### 2.1 Institutional and professional income — the AIIMS finding

**Established** — AIIMS faculty pay scales, applied to AIIMS Mangalagiri, which sits inside the Phase 1 recommended micro-location:

| Role | Published pay | Est. gross monthly | Annual | Reaches |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Senior Resident | ₹90,000 – 1,10,000/month | ₹0.9 – 1.1 L | ₹11 – 13 L | **Community A** |
| Assistant Professor | Pay Level 12, basic ₹1,01,500 + 20% NPA + DA | ~₹2.0 – 2.2 L | ₹24 – 26 L | **Community B**, edge of C |
| Additional Professor | Pay Level 13-A2, ₹1,48,200 – 2,11,400 + NPA + DA | ~₹3.0 – 4.3 L | ₹36 – 52 L | **Community C / D** |
| Professor, super-specialty | ₹3 – 4 lakh/month all-in | ₹3 – 4 L | ₹36 – 48 L | **Community D** |

**Established — and it corrects a natural assumption.** SRM University AP has a 100-acre campus at Neerukonda, and VIT-AP sits beside the Secretariat. It is tempting to treat their faculty as a premium housing pool. **The published pay does not support that.** SRM Assistant Professor pay averages roughly **₹57,000/month (~₹6.8 lakh p.a.)** — **below the Community A threshold.**

> **Established.** In this catchment, the high-income institution is **AIIMS**, not the private universities. Academic staff at SRM and VIT are a **rental and entry-housing** population, not a premium buyer pool. Any plan that treats "the university cluster" as one affluent segment is wrong on the numbers.

### 2.2 Government income

**Established** — AP has constituted a **Ninth Pay Revision Commission**. The **8th Central Pay Commission** was notified on 3 November 2025, effective 1 January 2026, with an expected **30–34% hike** and minimum basic rising from ₹18,000 toward ₹40,000–54,000; actual rollout is expected to extend into 2027 with retrospective arrears.

**Reasoned** — Government employment reaches **Community A comfortably and Community B at senior grades**. It does not reach C or D on salary alone. But two features make it disproportionately useful:
- **It is the most loan-friendly income in the catchment** — documented, stable, and lenders price it accordingly.
- **A pay revision with arrears is a purchase trigger.** A 30%+ revision with retrospective arrears creates exactly the lump sum that clears the down-payment barrier in §1.3. If the AP 9th PRC lands on a similar timeline, **2027 is a structurally favourable year for Community A and B sales to government households.**

### 2.3 Trade and business income — the deepest local pool

**Established** — Guntur's chilli, tobacco, cotton and pulses complex is described as a **USD 4–5 billion ecosystem** including forward linkages, with international standing in red chillies, tobacco and cotton, plus a textile base. Vijayawada is coastal Andhra's commercial capital.

**Established** — AP's income-tax filer base grew from **25.99 lakh (2023-24) to 28.70 lakh (2024-25), +10.42%**, reaching 30.76 lakh by February 2025 — roughly **5–6% of the state's population** filing returns.

**Reasoned** — Trading wealth in this belt is substantially **cash-generative and partly undocumented**. That produces a buyer who is *stronger* on the §1.3 cash barrier and *weaker* on the FOIR test — the mirror image of the salaried buyer. Practically: larger down payments, smaller loans, faster closes, and more sensitivity to registration value than to EMI.

### 2.4 The land-pooling landowners — the segment an outsider would miss

**Established** — Amaravati's land pooling took **33,008+ acres from roughly 25,000 farmers across ~29 villages.** Per acre surrendered, a farmer received **approximately 1,000 sq yd of residential land and 250–450 sq yd of commercial land** (some sources state 800–1,200 sq yd residential), plus an annuity of **₹30,000–50,000 per acre per year for ten years, escalating 10% annually**, plus social benefits including a farm loan waiver up to ₹1.5 lakh.

**Established** — Amaravati plot rates run **₹23,000–70,000 per sq yd** (Phase 2 §6.1).

**Computed** — A farmer who surrendered **one acre** holds roughly **1,000 sq yd residential at ₹23,000–70,000 = ₹2.3–7.0 crore**, plus commercial land worth **₹1 crore or more**. Average holding across the scheme is **~1.3 acres**.

> **Established. This is the most distinctive segment in the catchment, and it is structurally unlike every other one.**
>
> These households are **land-rich and cash-thin**: potentially ₹3–10 crore of land against an annuity of roughly ₹50,000–85,000 a year. They clear Community C and D on *assets* and fail on *income*.
>
> They cannot be sold to with a conventional EMI proposition. They can be sold to with **land-for-home exchange, joint development, or part-consideration-in-kind structures** — which is precisely what the `/land-owners` page found in Phase 4 §6.1 exists to do, and which few competitors offer.

### 2.5 Diaspora income

**Established** — The Telugu diaspora is estimated at **~3 million worldwide** by the AP government; an independent count gives ~2.3 million, of which **1,230,000 in the United States** and **388,000 in Saudi Arabia**, with further populations in Malaysia, Myanmar, Australia, Canada and the UK. **APNRTS, the state's NRT agency, is headquartered in Guntur** — inside the catchment.

**Established** — Phase 2 §7.2: NRI share of Indian real-estate investment rose from 10–12% (2019) to 17–19% (2024). Gulf remittance purpose splits **27% investment / 26% family support / 22% retirement planning**.

**Established — and this is the reality check.** A national NRI survey found that **60%+ of NRI respondents target Hyderabad, NCR or Bengaluru.** **Amaravati is not a top-tier NRI destination.** Any plan assuming NRI demand arrives on its own is wrong; NRI demand here has to be *created*, against three cities with better-known markets.

**Established** — NRIs can complete a purchase entirely remotely using a registered **Power of Attorney**, and RERA plus digital verification has made remote buying materially safer. The stated NRI concern is **fraud risk from inability to verify on the ground.**

**Reasoned — the split that matters.** US-based Telugu households (1.23 million, technology-weighted) and Gulf-based Telugu households (388,000 in Saudi alone, remittance-weighted) are **two different segments with two different products.** Treating "NRI" as one audience — which Phase 3 found every competitor doing — wastes the larger of the two.

## 3. The segments that actually exist — full profiles

### 3.0 Why the brief's fifteen labels collapse into eight segments

The fifteen hypotheses in the brief are not fifteen segments. They are **three different segmentation axes overlaid on each other**:

| Axis | Hypotheses using it |
| --- | --- |
| **Life stage** | First-time buyers · young professionals · young couples · growing families · upgraders |
| **Affluence** | Established business families · affluent professionals · HNIs · investors |
| **Geography** | Local buyers · Vijayawada · Guntur · Amaravati-region · outside AP · NRIs |

A "growing family" in Vijayawada is simultaneously a life-stage hypothesis, a geography hypothesis, and — depending on what the household does for a living — an affluence hypothesis. Profiling all fifteen would profile the same households three times and produce a segmentation that cannot be used to allocate a rupee of media spend, because the segments would not be mutually exclusive.

> **Established — the discriminator in this catchment is income *source*, not income *amount*.**
>
> §1.3 showed why. The binding constraint is **cash at purchase**, not documented income: ₹17.6 lakh for Community A rising to ₹74.1 lakh for Community D. Two households with identical ₹30 lakh annual incomes — one salaried, one trading — have completely different abilities to clear that gate, completely different documentation, completely different objections, and completely different decision timelines.
>
> So the primary axis is **where the money comes from.** Life stage and geography become descriptors *within* each segment rather than segments in their own right.

This produces **eight segments** that are mutually exclusive, individually addressable, and each traceable to a specific income source established in §2. §4 then returns to all fifteen original hypotheses and tests each one explicitly against this structure.

### 3.0.1 How to read the profiles

Each profile covers the twenty attributes the brief asks for. As a general rule:

- **Attributes 1–7** (demographics through financial capacity) are **Established** — computed from §1, or drawn from the income sources in §2.
- **Attributes 8–20** (motivation through influencers) are **Applied** or **Reasoned** — no primary research exists for this catchment, so these are directional and every one of them is a testable hypothesis. §7 lists the tests.

Where a specific line carries higher or lower confidence than its neighbours, it is labelled inline.

### Segment 1 — Vijayawada & Guntur trade and business families

*The deepest pool of purchase-ready cash in the catchment.*

**Size — Reasoned, order of magnitude only.** AP had **28.70 lakh ITR filers** in the latest year (up from 25.99 lakh). NTR and Guntur districts hold roughly 8% of the state's population but a disproportionate share of its filers, being urban and trade-centred. The order of magnitude of filing households across the two districts is therefore **low hundreds of thousands**; the slice clearing the ₹25 lakh household income *and* ₹52 lakh cash test for Community C is a **small fraction of that**. This is a bounding exercise, not a count — §7 flags it for verification against registration data.

| Attribute | Profile |
| --- | --- |
| **Demographics** | Principal buyer 38–58. Business owner, commission agent, wholesaler, transport operator, jeweller, hospitality, construction supplier. Education varies widely and correlates poorly with wealth. |
| **Geography** | Vijayawada city (Governorpet, Labbipet, Benz Circle, Patamata), Guntur town (Brodipet, Arundelpet, Lakshmipuram), Tenali, Mangalagiri town, Chilakaluripet. |
| **Income / purchasing power** | Household income frequently ₹25 lakh–1 crore+, but **partly undocumented**. Guntur's chilli, tobacco and cotton ecosystem alone moves **USD 4–5 billion** (Phase 1 §4). Purchasing power is materially higher than declared income. |
| **Family structure** | Joint or semi-joint. Two to four adults contributing. Often multiple related households buying in the same project. |
| **Life stage** | Consolidating. Children in senior school, college, or newly married. Frequently buying *for* the next generation, not for themselves. |
| **Housing requirement** | 3BHK large, 4BHK, or villa. **Space, parking for 2–4 vehicles, and a formal receiving area matter more than efficiency.** Vaastu compliance is close to non-negotiable. Servant provision assumed. |
| **Financial capacity** | **Clears Community C and D comfortably on cash.** The ₹52–74 lakh cash requirement in §1.2 is the easiest test in this document for this segment. Loan is used selectively — for tax reasons or to preserve working capital — not from necessity. |
| **Purchase motivation** | Three, in rough order: (1) **converting business cash into a hard, visible asset**; (2) **status confirmation** within a tight, observant peer network; (3) providing for children. Rental yield is close to irrelevant. |
| **Pain points** | Documentation and title anxiety — this segment has seen local disputes. Distrust of developers who "sell and disappear". Reluctance to expose financial detail to a bank. Concern that the family will be the only known name in an unknown project. |
| **Objections** | *"Who else has bought?"* is the first objection and the most important one in this entire document. Also: *"Will it actually get built?"*, *"What is the resale?"*, *"Why not just buy land?"* — land being this segment's default asset class. |
| **Purchase triggers** | A good trading season. A daughter's or son's marriage. A peer in the same trade buying first. A land sale. Guideline-value revisions (the **1 Feb 2026 revision**, Phase 1 §6) that make waiting more expensive. |
| **Decision factors** | Developer track record above everything. Then location legibility ("everyone knows where that is"), then plot/unit size, then who the neighbours will be. Amenity lists rank low. |
| **Emotional drivers** | Standing. Permanence. Being seen to have arrived — and being seen to have arrived *in the capital region*, which carries specific meaning here. Legacy for children. |
| **Rational drivers** | Land appreciation logic applied to built product. Capital preservation. Proximity to existing business premises — **this segment will not move far from its trade base.** |
| **Preferred channels** | **Referral and personal introduction first.** Then a physical site visit. Then print and hoardings for legitimacy. Digital is a *verification* channel here, not a discovery channel. |
| **Information sources** | Trade peers, family, the family's advocate and chartered accountant, local brokers, the sub-registrar network. **Reasoned — the CA and the advocate are gatekeepers, and neither is currently addressed by any competitor found in Phase 3.** |
| **Digital behaviour** | WhatsApp-primary. YouTube for long-form. Will search the developer's name and read the first page of results — Phase 4 established KMV's search visibility is thin, which matters most for *this* segment. Unlikely to fill a portal enquiry form; likely to call a number seen on a hoarding. |
| **Site-visit behaviour** | **Visits with family, usually 3–6 people, often unannounced, frequently on a Sunday.** Expects the decision-maker on site. Will visit two or three times before committing. Elders present on the confirming visit. |
| **Decision timeline** | **3–9 months.** Long consideration, then a sudden close once the family aligns. Deposit-to-agreement can be days. |
| **Influencers** | Spouse, father or eldest brother, the family CA, and one respected peer who has already bought. **Applied — Indian purchase research consistently finds the decision is collective rather than individual; here the collective is wider than the nuclear family.** |

> **Reasoned — the decisive insight.** This segment does not buy a project; it buys **the other buyers**. The first fifteen sales into a recognised local network determine whether the next hundred are easy or impossible. That has a direct consequence for launch sequencing which Phase 6 has to carry: **the premium tiers should be seeded before they are advertised.**

### Segment 2 — Senior institutional medical professionals

*The most reliably documented high income in the corridor, and the one most tied to the micro-location.*

**Size — Reasoned.** AIIMS Mangalagiri at full faculty strength, plus NRI Academy of Sciences at Chinakakani, Katuri Medical College, Guntur Medical College and the private hospital cluster in Vijayawada (Ramesh, Manipal, KIMS). The order of magnitude of households clearing Community B or above across all of these is **low thousands**; the slice clearing Community D is **a few hundred**. §7 flags this for verification.

| Attribute | Profile |
| --- | --- |
| **Demographics** | 32–55. Consultants, faculty, super-specialists, senior residents at the entry end. Often two doctors in one household — which changes the arithmetic completely. |
| **Geography** | Currently living in Mangalagiri, Chinakakani, Tadepalli and Vijayawada. **This segment already commutes across the exact corridor the project sits in.** |
| **Income / purchasing power** | §2.1: Senior Resident ₹11–13 L p.a. → Community A. Assistant Professor ₹24–26 L → Community B. Additional Professor ₹36–52 L → C/D. Super-specialty Professor ₹36–48 L → D. **A dual-doctor household at Assistant Professor level clears Community C.** |
| **Family structure** | Nuclear, one or two children, frequently with a dependent parent. Both adults working, often on incompatible shift patterns. |
| **Life stage** | Establishing or consolidating. Many are recent arrivals to the corridor — AIIMS Mangalagiri is a young institution — which makes them **actively in-market rather than latently in-market.** |
| **Housing requirement** | 3BHK or compact 4BHK. **Commute time to the hospital is the single hardest constraint** — a resident on call cannot live 40 minutes away. Security, a study, quiet, and reliable power rank above clubhouse scale. |
| **Financial capacity** | Highly loan-eligible: documented, stable, salaried, lender-favoured. **But cash-constrained** — a 38-year-old Additional Professor may clear the ₹94,800 EMI and still struggle with ₹52 lakh in hand. **This is the segment for which construction-linked and possession-linked payment plans matter most.** |
| **Purchase motivation** | Ending the commute. Converting rent into ownership. A permanent base after a career of institutional transfers. Schooling for children. |
| **Pain points** | Time — genuinely scarce, and this is not a figure of speech for a clinician. Cannot do multi-week diligence. Cannot attend weekday site visits. Frustrated by sales processes that require repetition. |
| **Objections** | *"Will it be delivered on time?"* — delay costs them rent and disruption simultaneously. *"Is the commute really 12 minutes?"* *"What are the schools like?"* *"Am I overpaying versus Vijayawada?"* |
| **Purchase triggers** | Confirmation in post or promotion from Senior Resident to faculty. A child reaching school age. Rent revision. Spouse's job confirmation. **A colleague buying in the same project — this is a small, highly connected professional community.** |
| **Decision factors** | Commute time, delivery credibility, build quality, school access, security. **Applied — Knight Frank's 2025 buyer work finds professional buyers weighting developer delivery record above amenity depth; that direction transfers even if the magnitude does not.** |
| **Emotional drivers** | Stability after years of transfers. Belonging in a place they moved to for work. Peer-appropriate address. Relief from logistics. |
| **Rational drivers** | Rent-to-EMI arithmetic. Loan eligibility they know they possess. Tax deduction. Commute distance measured, not described. |
| **Preferred channels** | **Digital-first, and this is the segment where that is actually true.** Search, portals, developer website, WhatsApp for follow-up. Highly responsive to precise, specific information; highly averse to sales pressure. |
| **Information sources** | Google, 99acres/MagicBricks/Housing, RERA registration lookup, colleague referral, resident WhatsApp groups. **Reasoned — this segment will check RERA. Most of the catchment will not.** |
| **Digital behaviour** | Researches at night and between shifts. Reads specification detail. Compares 3–5 projects on a spreadsheet. Will watch a 6-minute walkthrough. Responds to email; often *cannot* take calls during the day. |
| **Site-visit behaviour** | **Weekday-evening or Sunday, by appointment, couple only, one focused visit and one confirming visit.** Wants the drive from the hospital timed on the way. Bringing the family happens at the second visit. |
| **Decision timeline** | **6 weeks to 4 months** — the shortest of the affluent segments, because the trigger is usually dated (a posting, a school year, a lease expiry). |
| **Influencers** | Spouse — **co-equal, often the primary decision-maker on the home itself.** Then colleagues who have already bought, then parents on the financial question. |

> **Established — the structural link to Phase 4.** Phase 4 found that **KMV Projects built AIIMS Mangalagiri.** For the one segment that most values delivery credibility and whose workplace *is* that building, this is not a marketing claim to be written — it is a verifiable fact sitting three kilometres from the site. No competitor in the Phase 3 set has anything comparable to say to this audience.

### Segment 3 — US and developed-market Telugu diaspora households

*The largest latent pool, the smallest active one. The gap between those two is the entire opportunity.*

**Size — Established at the pool level, Reasoned at the addressable level.** **1,230,000 Telugu-origin people in the United States**, plus populations in Canada, Australia and the UK — roughly **400,000 US households**. Coastal Andhra is a major source region for this migration. **But 60%+ of NRI buyers target Hyderabad, NCR or Bengaluru** (§2.5). The *active* Amaravati-considering slice is small and must be created rather than harvested.

| Attribute | Profile |
| --- | --- |
| **Demographics** | 33–52. Technology, healthcare, finance, academia. H-1B, green card and naturalised citizens. Household income in USD, frequently USD 150,000–400,000. |
| **Geography** | New Jersey, the Bay Area, Dallas, Chicago, Atlanta, Seattle. **Family origin in Krishna, Guntur, West Godavari, Prakasam** — origin district matters far more than current city for targeting. |
| **Income / purchasing power** | **Clears Community D on income without strain.** The constraint is not affordability; it is *allocation* — this purchase competes with a US mortgage, college funds, and Hyderabad property. |
| **Family structure** | Nuclear abroad, with **parents still resident in AP** — usually in Vijayawada, Guntur or the home village. That parental household is central to everything below. |
| **Life stage** | Settled abroad. Ten to twenty years out. Parents ageing. Beginning to think about a permanent Indian anchor. |
| **Housing requirement** | Villa or large premium apartment. **Lock-and-leave security is the defining requirement** — the home will be empty for 11 months a year. Maintenance handled by someone else. Space for parents. Guest capacity for the annual visit. |
| **Financial capacity** | Very strong. **NRE/NRO repatriation rules, PoA execution and FEMA compliance are the friction, not the money.** Purchase can be completed entirely remotely under a registered Power of Attorney. |
| **Purchase motivation** | Four, and they are not the same: (1) **a home for ageing parents**; (2) **a personal return/retirement anchor**; (3) **currency-hedged asset allocation**; (4) **identity and belonging** — a stake in the state capital. Note that (1) makes the parents the actual occupant. |
| **Pain points** | **Cannot verify anything personally.** Cannot inspect. Depends on a relative for site visits — and that relative's opinion becomes decisive. Time-zone friction in every conversation. Has heard the horror stories. |
| **Objections** | *"How do I know this is real?"* — the fraud objection, which §2.5 established is the stated primary NRI concern. Then: *"Who maintains it when it's empty?"*, *"Can I sell it if I never return?"*, *"Why Amaravati and not Hyderabad?"* — **this last one is the real competitor, and it is a city, not a project.** |
| **Purchase triggers** | An India visit — **December–January and the summer are the two windows, and they are non-negotiable.** A parent's health event. A green card or citizenship milestone. A bonus or vesting event. A sibling buying. |
| **Decision factors** | Developer credibility and legal cleanliness above all. Then remote-management capability. Then rental/resale exit. Then the capital-city story. **Amenities matter less than they do to any other segment** because the household will not use them. |
| **Emotional drivers** | Guilt and duty toward parents. Nostalgia for the region. Wanting to be *part of the capital's making*, not to observe it from abroad. Standing within the extended family. |
| **Rational drivers** | INR/USD asset diversification. Capital appreciation on a capital-city thesis. A physical base for eventual return. Rental income while empty. |
| **Preferred channels** | **Digital, entirely.** Search, YouTube, LinkedIn, Facebook and WhatsApp community groups, Telugu-diaspora association networks, video call. **APNRTS is headquartered in Guntur — inside the catchment — and is an institutional channel no Phase 3 competitor was found using.** |
| **Information sources** | Google in English, YouTube walkthroughs, diaspora WhatsApp and Facebook groups, family in India, NRI-focused property content, RERA verification. |
| **Digital behaviour** | **Very high-intent, very long research cycles, very low form-fill rates.** Watches long video. Reads at odd hours relative to India. Will do a video site walkthrough before a physical one. Expects to be able to transact digitally. |
| **Site-visit behaviour** | **A proxy visit by a relative first, then a personal visit compressed into a single India trip.** The trip visit is decisive and there is no second chance — if the site is not ready to close in that window, the sale is lost for a year. |
| **Decision timeline** | **6–18 months of consideration, then a 5-day decision window during the India visit.** This bimodal pattern is the single most important operational fact about this segment. |
| **Influencers** | **Spouse, then the parents in India, then the sibling or cousin who does the physical inspection.** That inspecting relative holds effective veto and is almost never the person the marketing is aimed at. |

> **Reasoned — the two structural mistakes to avoid.**
> 1. **Treating "NRI" as one segment.** §2.5 established the US and Gulf pools are different in size, income, purpose and product fit. Phase 3 found every competitor collapsing them.
> 2. **Campaigning to the diaspora on Indian seasonality.** This segment's buying window is set by *their* travel calendar — December–January and summer — not by the Indian festive quarter. A launch calendar built on Dasara–Diwali will miss them.

### Segment 4 — Amaravati land-pooling landowner households

*Structurally unlike every other segment in this document, and the one no competitor is built to serve.*

**Size — Established.** **33,008+ acres pooled from approximately 25,000 farmer households**, average holding ~1.3 acres. **Reasoned:** the tier holding one acre or more — order of 12,000–15,000 households — holds **₹2.3–7.0 crore in residential returnable plots** plus commercial land, against an annuity of roughly ₹50,000–85,000 a year.

| Attribute | Profile |
| --- | --- |
| **Demographics** | 40–70 principally, with sons and daughters aged 25–45 increasingly driving decisions. Agricultural background; many now without an operating farm, the land having been surrendered. |
| **Geography** | **The 29 villages of the capital area** — Thullur, Velagapudi, Rayapudi, Mandadam, Nelapadu, Lingayapalem, Venkatapalem, Nidamarru, Kuragallu, Neerukonda and others. **They live on top of the recommended micro-location.** |
| **Income / purchasing power** | **The defining asymmetry: land-rich, cash-thin.** Assets of ₹3–10 crore. Annual cash income often under ₹2 lakh from the annuity. **Clears Community C and D on net worth; fails Community A on income.** |
| **Family structure** | Joint, multi-generational, often 6–12 people across three generations in one compound. Decisions are made by the family, not the individual. |
| **Life stage** | **In forced transition.** The occupation is gone, the land is committed, and the returnable plots are being handed over. This is an entire community re-deciding how it lives — a once-in-a-generation moment that will not recur. |
| **Housing requirement** | **Two distinct needs.** (a) The parent generation wants ground-connected living, space, and continuity with village life — a villa, not a flat. (b) The 25–45 generation, now urban-employed or aspiring, wants a serviced apartment with security and amenities. **A single household may need both.** |
| **Financial capacity** | **The conventional structure does not work.** No income statement, no ITR history, poor loan eligibility, and no need for a loan. **What works: land-for-home exchange, joint development on the returnable plot, part-consideration in kind, or a plot-plus-cash swap against a finished unit.** |
| **Purchase motivation** | Converting an illiquid, undevelopable plot into a **finished, income-generating or liveable asset** — without becoming a developer. Providing separate homes for sons. Re-establishing status in a landscape where the old marker of status (land) has been surrendered. |
| **Pain points** | **Deep, evidence-based institutional distrust** — the pooling process itself, the capital's stop-start history since 2019, and the years of uncertainty before the April 2026 gazette notification. Fear of being outmanoeuvred a second time. No experience of formal property transactions of this type. |
| **Objections** | *"What exactly do I get, and when?"* *"What happens if you don't build?"* *"Why should I hand over land again?"* — **this last objection is emotionally loaded in a way no brochure answers, and it is the correct objection to have.** |
| **Purchase triggers** | Plot handover completion. A neighbour completing an exchange successfully. A son's marriage. A specific, written, legally structured offer — **this segment does not respond to advertising; it responds to a document.** |
| **Decision factors** | Terms of exchange, legal enforceability, developer's local standing, and **precedent — has anyone in the village done this and been treated well?** |
| **Emotional drivers** | Dignity. Vindication after a decade of uncertainty. Being a participant in the capital rather than a casualty of it. Provision for sons. **Handled carelessly, this segment is not just a lost sale but a local reputational liability.** |
| **Rational drivers** | Asset conversion without capital outlay. Avoiding the cost, risk and expertise of self-development. Income from a portion while retaining a portion. |
| **Preferred channels** | **In-person, in the village, in Telugu.** Village elders, farmer associations, the local advocate, the surveyor. **Not digital. Not advertising.** A land-owner relations function, not a marketing campaign. |
| **Information sources** | Neighbours, family, the village network, the CRDA office, local Telugu press and Telugu YouTube channels. |
| **Digital behaviour** | Telugu-language YouTube and WhatsApp forwarding are genuinely high in this group; formal web research is not. The younger generation researches on behalf of the elders. |
| **Site-visit behaviour** | **Does not need to visit — they live there.** What they need instead is to see **completed work**: a finished building, a delivered promise, someone they know who was paid what they were told they would be paid. |
| **Decision timeline** | **6–24 months.** Slow, consultative, and irreversible once made. Trust accrues over repeated contact, not over a campaign flight. |
| **Influencers** | The eldest male, the sons, the village elder, the family advocate, and **the first neighbour to complete a transaction — who becomes the single most persuasive asset available.** |

> **Established — this is a defensible white space, and Phase 4 already found the door.**
>
> Phase 3 §5 identified land-owner engagement as a surviving white space: no competitor in the screened set offers a structured land-for-home proposition. Phase 4 §6.1 found KMV Spaces already publishes a **`/land-owners` page**.
>
> The asset exists and is unexploited. **Reasoned:** this segment does not primarily buy Community A–D units off a price list — it enters through an *exchange structure* that supplies land, absorbs inventory, and creates local advocacy at the same time. It is a **supply-side and demand-side segment simultaneously**, which is why it is worth more than its unit count suggests.

### Segment 5 — Government, public-sector and the relocating capital workforce

*The most loan-eligible income in the catchment, and the one with a dated trigger attached to it.*

**Size — Insufficient public evidence.** No published headcount was found for the number of state employees who will relocate to Amaravati following the **6 April 2026 gazette notification** making it the sole capital by Act of Parliament (Phase 1 §1). Estimates circulate; none traced to a primary source. §7 flags this as a priority gap, because the number materially changes Community A and B sizing.

| Attribute | Profile |
| --- | --- |
| **Demographics** | 30–52. State government officers, central government staff, PSU and bank employees, teachers, police, judiciary support staff. Grade determines everything about the profile. |
| **Geography** | Currently Vijayawada, Guntur, Tadepalli and Mangalagiri; a portion still in Hyderabad and relocating. |
| **Income / purchasing power** | §2.2: reaches **Community A comfortably, Community B at senior grades**, and does not reach C or D on salary alone. Dual-government households and those with agricultural land alongside are the exception and reach further. |
| **Family structure** | Nuclear with one or two children, frequently supporting parents. Single earner in many cases; dual-government households are common and change the arithmetic. |
| **Life stage** | Establishing to consolidating. Many have been in rented accommodation through a decade of capital uncertainty and have deferred buying because of it. |
| **Housing requirement** | 2BHK and 3BHK. Gated security, reliable water and power, school access, a covered car park. **Efficient carpet area matters more than showpiece amenity** — the budget is real and the household knows it precisely. |
| **Financial capacity** | **The most loan-friendly income in this document.** Documented, stable, pensionable, lender-preferred, often with subsidised institutional loan access. **The constraint is the down payment, not the EMI** — ₹17.6 lakh for Community A, ₹31.5 lakh for B. |
| **Purchase motivation** | Ending a long rental period. Buying before the capital's confirmation is fully priced in. Proximity to a workplace that is itself moving. Children's schooling. |
| **Pain points** | The down payment. Transfer risk — a posting can move them. A decade of watching the capital start, stop and restart, which has trained caution. Genuine sensitivity to ₹200–300/sq ft differences. |
| **Objections** | *"Can I afford the down payment this year?"* *"What if I'm transferred?"* *"Is this priced above Vijayawada for the same thing?"* *"Will possession actually be in 2029?"* |
| **Purchase triggers** | **The pay revision.** §2.2: the 8th CPC is effective 1 January 2026 with a 30–34% expected hike, and rollout with retrospective arrears is expected to extend into 2027; AP's 9th PRC is constituted. **Arrears are exactly the lump sum that clears the down-payment gate.** Also: confirmation of posting to Amaravati, promotion, a child reaching school age. |
| **Decision factors** | Price per square foot, EMI affordability, possession date, commute, RERA registration, school access. **This segment compares on a spreadsheet and will find a ₹150/sq ft difference.** |
| **Emotional drivers** | Security. Ending the rent cycle. Respectability of address. Pride in the capital as a personal, not abstract, project. |
| **Rational drivers** | Rent-versus-EMI. Loan eligibility. Section 24 and 80C deductions. Buying ahead of an anticipated price rise. |
| **Preferred channels** | Mixed. Portals and search for shortlisting; **WhatsApp and departmental word-of-mouth for validation.** Print retains real credibility here. Employer-adjacent and association channels are unusually effective and unusually cheap. |
| **Information sources** | Colleagues, portals, the developer site, RERA, local Telugu press, family. |
| **Digital behaviour** | Portal-led. Will submit enquiry forms — **this is one of the few segments that reliably will.** Responds to EMI calculators and price transparency. Mobile-first, Telugu and English mixed. |
| **Site-visit behaviour** | **Sunday, with spouse and children, comparing two or three projects in one day.** Price-sheet driven. Will ask for the all-in number including registration, and should be given it. |
| **Decision timeline** | **3–8 months**, compressing sharply when arrears or a bonus lands. |
| **Influencers** | Spouse — decisive. Colleagues who have bought. Parents on the down payment, which they often part-fund. |

> **Reasoned — the timing point that should shape the launch calendar.** A 30%+ pay revision with retrospective arrears is a **once-in-a-decade liquidity event for the exact households that Community A and B need.** If the AP 9th PRC lands on a comparable timeline, the arrears window is the single most valuable dated opportunity in this document — and it is a *known* date, not a forecast. Phase 6 should build around it rather than discover it.

### Segment 6 — Vijayawada salaried professionals and apartment upgraders

*Numerically the largest local segment, and the one facing the most competition.*

**Size — Reasoned.** Phase 3 counted approximately **3,269 mid-market apartment units** in the screened competitive set alone, against roughly **420 villa units** — an **8:1 ratio.** The existing Vijayawada apartment stock these households would be upgrading *from* is far larger again. The pool is deep; so is the supply aimed at it.

| Attribute | Profile |
| --- | --- |
| **Demographics** | 30–45. IT services, banking, insurance, pharma distribution, logistics, education administration, healthcare non-clinical. Graduate or postgraduate. |
| **Geography** | Vijayawada — Benz Circle, Poranki, Gollapudi, Kanuru, Auto Nagar, Ramavarappadu; and Tadepalli. **Reasoned: the psychological barrier for this segment is the Krishna river, not the distance.** Moving to Mangalagiri is a genuine relocation decision, not a neighbourhood change. |
| **Income / purchasing power** | ₹12–25 lakh household, frequently dual-income. **Clears Community A, reaches Community B when both earn.** Does not reach C or D. |
| **Family structure** | Nuclear. One or two children under 12. Often supporting or co-residing with a parent. |
| **Life stage** | Growing family, currently in a 2BHK, needing a 3BHK. **This is the classic upgrader, and it is a real segment here — but a contested one.** |
| **Housing requirement** | 3BHK, 1,200–1,700 sq ft. Gated community, children's play area, security, parking, power backup, lift reliability. School within a reasonable drive. |
| **Financial capacity** | Loan-dependent — **Applied: 79% of urban Indian buyers use a home loan (Knight Frank, 2025).** Down payment is usually funded by a mix of savings, provident fund and parental contribution. Existing apartment sale sometimes funds the upgrade, which introduces a **resale dependency** — and Phase 2 §10 found resale in this corridor is not liquid. |
| **Purchase motivation** | Space. A better environment for children. Escaping an ageing, unmanaged building. Status progression from "flat" to "gated community". |
| **Pain points** | Selling the existing apartment. Commute lengthening if they move south. Schooling continuity. Budget stretch. Construction-period double outgo — rent plus EMI. |
| **Objections** | *"It's too far from my office."* *"What about the children's school?"* *"Can I sell my current flat at a fair price?"* *"Why pay a premium over Vijayawada?"* — **this last is the central objection for this segment and the hardest one in the entire plan to answer honestly.** |
| **Purchase triggers** | A second child. A promotion or job change. The existing building's maintenance failing. A school admission. Landlord rent revision. |
| **Decision factors** | Price, size, possession date, commute, school access, developer credibility, and **crucially — comparison against a Vijayawada alternative at a lower per-square-foot rate.** |
| **Emotional drivers** | Doing better for the children. Peer parity — friends who have already moved to gated communities. A sense of arriving in the middle class properly. |
| **Rational drivers** | Per-square-foot value, appreciation potential from the capital story, EMI within reach, maintenance quality. |
| **Preferred channels** | **Portals and social. The most conventionally reachable segment in this document** — and therefore the most expensively contested, since every Phase 3 competitor is buying the same inventory. |
| **Information sources** | 99acres, MagicBricks, Housing, Facebook and Instagram, YouTube walkthroughs, colleagues, brokers. |
| **Digital behaviour** | High. Fills forms, compares on portals, watches walkthroughs, joins project WhatsApp groups, reads Google reviews. **Applied — this is the behaviour national buyer research describes, and it transfers to this segment better than to any other here.** |
| **Site-visit behaviour** | **Weekend, with spouse and children, three to five projects across two or three weekends.** Highly comparison-driven. Show-flat quality matters disproportionately because it is the only tangible evidence available. |
| **Decision timeline** | **4–10 months**, often extended by the dependency on selling the existing home. |
| **Influencers** | Spouse — co-equal and frequently primary. Parents on funding. Colleagues and friends who have made the same move. |

> **Reasoned — the honest position.** This segment is real, large and reachable, and it is where **Phase 3's crowding finding bites hardest**: roughly 3,269 competing units aimed at broadly this household. Winning here on generic mid-market positioning means winning a price war. The defensible play is not to out-shout the competitive set for this segment but to give it a **specific, non-price reason** — commute to the institutional cluster, an integrated master plan of a scale the 8:1 apartment glut does not offer, or a delivery credential the competitors cannot claim.

### Segment 7 — Hyderabad-resident Telugu households with Andhra roots

*The domestic diaspora. Larger and closer than the international one, and almost entirely unaddressed.*

**Size — Reasoned.** Hyderabad holds a very large population of coastal-Andhra-origin households, many with continuing family, land and social ties in the Krishna–Guntur belt. No published count was found isolating this group as property buyers; §7 flags it. Directionally it is **larger than the US diaspora pool and far cheaper to reach.**

| Attribute | Profile |
| --- | --- |
| **Demographics** | 35–58. IT, pharma, business owners, professionals. Settled in Hyderabad for 10–30 years, frequently with parents still in AP. **Note: KMV Spaces LLP is itself registered in Ameerpet, Hyderabad (Phase 4) — the developer already sits inside this segment's home city.** |
| **Geography** | Hyderabad — Gachibowli, Madhapur, Kukatpally, Miyapur, Manikonda, LB Nagar. Origin districts across coastal Andhra. |
| **Income / purchasing power** | ₹25 lakh–1 crore+. **Clears Community C and D.** Already owns Hyderabad property, so this is an *additional* allocation rather than a first home. |
| **Family structure** | Nuclear in Hyderabad, extended family in AP. Ageing parents in Vijayawada, Guntur or the home town. |
| **Life stage** | Established. Peak earning years. Beginning to think about parents, retirement, and where the family's centre of gravity should be. |
| **Housing requirement** | Villa or premium apartment. **Weekend and periodic use, with parents as potential permanent occupants.** Lock-and-leave. Serviced maintenance. |
| **Financial capacity** | Strong. Domestic, so no FEMA or repatriation friction; **can transact in person over a weekend, which the international diaspora cannot.** |
| **Purchase motivation** | A base near ageing parents. Re-establishing a family presence in the home region. **A capital-city bet made by people who understand exactly what Hyderabad's growth did to those who bought early** — this is a segment with direct, lived experience of the thesis being sold. Retirement optionality. |
| **Pain points** | Absentee ownership and maintenance. Not knowing the current Amaravati market despite knowing the region. Being sold to as an outsider in a place they consider home. |
| **Objections** | *"Is Amaravati actually going to happen this time?"* — a question shaped by having watched 2015–2019 and then the reversal. *"Who looks after it?"* *"Is this better than adding to my Hyderabad portfolio?"* |
| **Purchase triggers** | **A family visit — festivals, weddings, funerals, all of which bring this segment back into the catchment on predictable dates.** A parent's health event. A Hyderabad property exit. Retirement within sight. |
| **Decision factors** | Credibility of the capital story, developer track record, maintenance model, distance from parents, resale depth. |
| **Emotional drivers** | Roots. Duty to parents. Wanting a stake in the capital of their own state. Some quiet competitive feeling relative to Hyderabad, which is worth understanding and not worth exploiting crudely. |
| **Rational drivers** | Appreciation on a capital thesis they have personally seen play out once. Diversification outside the Hyderabad market. A retirement asset acquired at Tier-2 pricing. |
| **Preferred channels** | **Digital, plus physical presence in Hyderabad.** Search, social, Telugu digital media. **Reasoned: a Hyderabad-side sales presence — an event, a lounge, a partner office — is a cheap, high-yield move that Phase 3 found no competitor making.** |
| **Information sources** | Telugu news and YouTube, family in AP, portals, professional and community networks in Hyderabad. |
| **Digital behaviour** | High. Bilingual. Consumes Telugu news and business content heavily. Reachable by geography-plus-affinity targeting far more precisely than most segments. |
| **Site-visit behaviour** | **Combines the visit with a family trip — a 4–5 hour drive or a short flight, typically Friday to Sunday.** Expects the visit to be worth the journey, which raises the bar on site readiness considerably. |
| **Decision timeline** | **4–12 months.** Slower than a resident buyer because nothing forces it — there is no lease expiry and no commute to fix. **The absence of urgency is this segment's defining commercial problem.** |
| **Influencers** | Spouse, parents in AP, siblings, and Hyderabad peers who have made similar investments. |

> **Reasoned — why this segment is underrated.** It has the diaspora's financial capacity and emotional pull without the diaspora's verification problem, time-zone friction or once-a-year buying window. It can drive down on a Saturday. Phase 3 found the competitive set marketing almost entirely to the local catchment; **the 4–5 hour radius is open.**

### Segment 8 — Gulf-based Telugu remittance households

*Real, but smaller in ticket than its headline numbers suggest — and it needs a different product.*

**Size — Established at the pool level.** **388,000 Telugu-origin people in Saudi Arabia** alone, with substantial populations across the UAE, Qatar, Kuwait and Oman. **Established:** Gulf remittance purpose splits **27% investment / 26% family support / 22% retirement planning** (Phase 2 §7.2).

| Attribute | Profile |
| --- | --- |
| **Demographics** | 28–50. Skilled and semi-skilled trades, technicians, supervisors, nursing, hospitality, driving, retail management. A smaller professional tier. |
| **Geography** | Saudi Arabia, UAE, Qatar, Kuwait, Oman. Origin heavily in coastal AP and Telangana districts. |
| **Income / purchasing power** | **Materially lower than the US pool, and tax-free but time-limited.** Realistically reaches **Community A and Community B**; Community C only for the professional tier or with pooled family funds. **Treating Gulf NRIs as a premium villa audience is an error — and Phase 3 found competitors making exactly that conflation.** |
| **Family structure** | **Frequently split — the earner abroad, spouse and children in India with grandparents.** That resident family is the actual occupant and, often, the actual decision-maker. |
| **Life stage** | Mid-career abroad with a finite horizon. Gulf employment is not permanent; **most intend to return.** Children's education in India is a live concern. |
| **Housing requirement** | 2BHK and 3BHK apartments. **Security for a family living without the male earner present is the single most important attribute** — more than amenity, more than size. School access. Ready or near-ready possession preferred. |
| **Financial capacity** | Savings-led rather than income-led. **Loan eligibility is weaker than the US pool** — foreign income, shorter permissible tenures, more documentation. Down payment usually accumulated over years and paid in tranches. |
| **Purchase motivation** | **Getting the family out of rented accommodation and into a secure, owned home** — this is the dominant motive and it is closer to Segment 5's than to Segment 3's. Then retirement planning (22%), then investment (27%). |
| **Pain points** | Verification from abroad, as with all diaspora. Limited leave — often 30 days a year or less. Payment scheduling against an irregular savings pattern. Real anxiety about the family's safety in the interim. |
| **Objections** | *"Can my wife handle this alone?"* *"What if my contract ends?"* *"Is possession certain?"* — **delay risk is felt more acutely here than anywhere else, because a delay means the family stays in rented accommodation without him.** |
| **Purchase triggers** | Contract renewal or a raise. Annual leave — **the visit is the buying window.** A child reaching school age. Accumulated savings crossing the down-payment line. A returning colleague's purchase. |
| **Decision factors** | Total price and payment flexibility, possession certainty, security, school proximity, family's approval. |
| **Emotional drivers** | Providing from a distance. Ending the separation with a permanent home to return to. Proof to the extended family that the years abroad were worth it. |
| **Rational drivers** | Rent elimination. A retirement asset. Currency advantage. Fixed EMI against rising rent. |
| **Preferred channels** | **WhatsApp above everything.** Telugu-language YouTube and Facebook. Regional community associations in the Gulf. **The resident family in India must be marketed to in parallel — they will do the visiting.** |
| **Information sources** | Family in India, Gulf-based Telugu community groups, YouTube in Telugu, WhatsApp forwards, returning friends. |
| **Digital behaviour** | Mobile-only, Telugu-language, WhatsApp-centric, video over text. Low tolerance for English-language brochure content. Will not fill a desktop web form. |
| **Site-visit behaviour** | **The family in India visits; the earner sees it on a video call.** The physical visit by the buyer happens during annual leave and is often confirmatory rather than exploratory. |
| **Decision timeline** | **9–24 months** — long accumulation, then a decision compressed into a leave window. |
| **Influencers** | **Spouse in India — decisive, because she will live there.** Parents. Community peers in the Gulf. |

> **Established — the product correction.** §2.5 and the table above make the same point from two directions. The Gulf pool is large but its ticket capacity sits at **Community A and B**, while the US pool sits at **C and D**. A single "NRI campaign" pointed at villas will spend the Gulf budget on an audience that cannot buy the product, and under-serve the audience that can. **They are two segments, two products, two channel plans and two languages.**

## 4. Testing the fifteen hypotheses in the brief

The brief listed fifteen possible segments and was explicit that they are **hypotheses, not predetermined audiences**. Each is now tested against §1 (the financial gate), §2 (the income sources that exist) and §3 (the eight segments those two produce).

| # | Hypothesis | Verdict | Reasoning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **First-time buyers** | **Real, but a descriptor — not a segment** | Cuts across S2 (senior residents), S5 (government), S6 (upgraders' younger cousins) and S8 (Gulf). All are first-time buyers; none behave alike. Useful for messaging, useless for targeting. |
| 2 | **Young professionals** | **Largely fails the gate as a single-earner group** | §1.2: Community A needs ₹11–14 L household income *and* ₹17.6 L cash. §2.1: SRM Assistant Professor pay is **~₹6.8 L p.a.** A single young professional in this catchment mostly does not clear it. Real only when dual-income or parent-funded. |
| 3 | **Young couples** | **Real — but only the dual-income version** | The strong case is the **dual-doctor household** (S2), where two Assistant Professor incomes clear Community C. A single-income young couple sits below Community A. **The variable is not age; it is the second income.** |
| 4 | **Growing families** | **Real — this is S6, plus part of S5** | The classic 2BHK-to-3BHK upgrade. Genuinely the largest local pool, and the most contested: Phase 3 counted ~3,269 competing units aimed at it. |
| 5 | **Upgraders** | **Real — S6, with a caveat that matters** | Many upgraders must **sell an existing apartment to fund the move**, and Phase 2 §10 found corridor resale is not liquid. Their purchase carries a dependency the developer does not control. |
| 6 | **Established business families** | **Real, and the strongest single segment — S1** | Clears the cash gate more easily than any other resident group. Deepest local money pool (Phase 1 §4). Buys the top two communities. |
| 7 | **Affluent professionals** | **Real — S2, plus senior grades of S5 and S7** | But "affluent professional" in this catchment means **doctor**, not academic (§2.1) and not, on salary alone, government officer (§2.2). The label needs that precision or it produces the wrong media plan. |
| 8 | **HNIs** | **Not a separate segment here — and the product is not an HNI product** | Two reasons. First, the households in this catchment meeting an HNI definition **are already** S1, S3 or S4 — there is no independent HNI population with distinct behaviour. Second, **Community D tops out at ₹2.57 crore.** Nationally that is an upper-affluent ticket, not a high-net-worth one. Positioning the project to "HNIs" would promise a tier the price sheet does not deliver. |
| 9 | **NRIs** | **Real, but rejected as a single segment** | Splits into **S3 (US/developed markets — Community C and D)** and **S8 (Gulf — Community A and B)**. §2.5 and §3 S8 establish they differ in income, purpose, product fit, language and channel. Phase 3 found every competitor collapsing them into one. |
| 10 | **Investors** | **Real but LOW priority — and actively risky in volume** | Three pieces of evidence. Phase 2 §9 found rental yields of **2.2–3.2%**, including a **yield inversion in Tadepalli at 2.2%** — premium stock earning less than cheaper stock. Phase 2 §10 found resale is **not liquid**. And the local investor's default asset is **land**, which offers better appreciation with no maintenance. **Beyond a modest share, investor buyers create a resale book that competes against the developer's own unsold inventory at handover.** |
| 11 | **Local buyers** | **Too broad to be a segment** | Resolves into four different segments with nothing in common: S1 (trade), S4 (landowners), S5 (government), S6 (salaried). Their only shared attribute is a postcode. |
| 12 | **Vijayawada buyers** | **A geography, not a segment** | Maps to S1 at the top, S6 in the middle, S5 alongside. **The useful Vijayawada-specific finding is not who they are but what stops them: the Krishna river is a psychological boundary, and crossing it is treated as relocation rather than a move.** |
| 13 | **Guntur buyers** | **A geography — and an under-rated one** | Maps to S1 and S5. **Worth separating from Vijayawada for one reason:** the Phase 1 recommended micro-location — the southern gateway of Mangalagiri mandal toward Chinakakani and Kaza — is **on the Guntur side of the corridor**, and involves no river crossing. Phase 3 found competitor marketing heavily Vijayawada-weighted. |
| 14 | **Amaravati-region buyers** | **Real — and it is S4, which is unlike anything else here** | The capital-area villages are not a generic local audience. They are ~25,000 landowner households in a once-in-a-generation asset transition, needing an exchange structure rather than a price list. |
| 15 | **Buyers outside Andhra Pradesh** | **Splits — one large real segment, one negligible one** | **Real: S7, Hyderabad-resident Telugu households with AP roots** — large, wealthy, four hours away, emotionally invested, and unaddressed by the Phase 3 competitive set. **Negligible: non-Telugu out-of-state buyers.** No evidence was found of meaningful non-Telugu demand for Amaravati residential product, and no plausible mechanism for it at this stage of the city's development. |

### 4.1 One hypothesis the brief did not list, tested anyway

**Private-university faculty and staff (SRM AP, VIT-AP).** The corridor narrative treats the university cluster as an affluent housing catchment. **§2.1 establishes it is not:** SRM Assistant Professor pay averaging **~₹57,000/month** sits below the Community A threshold. This population is a **rental and entry-housing** market. It is worth knowing about — it supports the corridor's rental economy and therefore the investor case — but it is not a buyer segment for this product.

### 4.2 What the test changed

Three findings survive as **corrections to the intuitive reading of the brief**:

1. **"NRI" is two segments, not one** — and the larger one by headcount (Gulf) buys the cheaper product, while the smaller one (US) buys the expensive one. Getting this backwards wastes the entire diaspora budget.
2. **"HNI" is not a segment in this catchment, and Community D is not an HNI product.** ₹2.57 crore is the ceiling.
3. **The largest and most obvious segment — growing families upgrading in Vijayawada — is the most crowded.** Phase 3's 8:1 apartment-to-villa ratio is aimed almost entirely at them. Being obvious and being winnable are not the same thing.

## 5. Prioritisation — HIGH, SECONDARY, LOW

### 5.1 The criteria, and why these six

Ranking segments by size alone would put the most crowded segment first. Ranking by ticket alone would put the smallest first. The six criteria below are chosen because each one, on the evidence of Phases 1–4, can independently kill a segment:

| Criterion | Weight | Why it can kill a segment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Revenue potential** | 25% | Plausible rupee contribution to the ₹506 Cr. A segment that buys only Community A cannot carry the project. |
| **Clears the cash gate** | 20% | §1.3 — the binding constraint is cash at purchase, not income. Segments that fail here fail regardless of desire. |
| **Addressable size** | 15% | A perfect-fit segment of 200 households cannot absorb 397 units. |
| **Reachability** | 15% | Cost and certainty of reaching them. S4 cannot be reached by advertising at all. |
| **Low competitive intensity** | 15% | Phase 3's crowding finding. Winning a contested segment costs multiples of winning an uncontested one. |
| **KMV-specific advantage** | 10% | Phase 4 found two: the **AIIMS Mangalagiri construction credential** and the existing **land-owner proposition**. An advantage that applies to one segment and not others should shift priority. |

Each segment is scored 1–5 per criterion. **Reasoned throughout** — the scores are my judgement applied to Phase 1–4 evidence, not measured values. They are shown so they can be argued with rather than hidden inside a conclusion.

### 5.3 The second axis — conversion horizon

A single score would hide something important. **The two highest-scoring segments are also the two slowest to convert.**

| Horizon | Segments | Typical decision timeline |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Near — convertible within a launch window** | S2, S5, S1, S6 | 6 weeks – 10 months |
| **Mid — convertible within a year, on their calendar not ours** | S3, S7 | 4 – 18 months, gated by travel |
| **Long — relationship-built, revenue late** | S4, S8 | 9 – 24 months |

> **Reasoned — the consequence.** Strategic value and cash timing are different questions. **A segment can be first in importance and last in revenue.** S4 is the clearest case: highest KMV-specific advantage, lowest competition, and the slowest possible conversion. Deferring it because it is slow guarantees it is never ready; treating it as a launch-quarter revenue line guarantees disappointment. **It has to start early and be measured late.**

### 5.4 The classification

#### HIGH PRIORITY

**S1 — Vijayawada & Guntur trade and business families.** *Score 4.05, near horizon.*
The deepest pool of purchase-ready cash in the catchment, buying the two communities that carry **61% of project revenue**. Clears the ₹52–74 lakh cash gate more easily than any other resident segment. Not the cheapest to reach — it runs on referral, not advertising — but nothing else combines this capacity with this proximity. **The single most important operational implication in this document belongs to this segment: seed the premium tiers into a recognised local network before advertising them.**

**S2 — Senior institutional medical professionals.** *Score 3.90, near horizon — the fastest converting segment here.*
Highest reachability of any segment, the only genuinely digital-first audience, dated purchase triggers, and a **KMV-specific advantage that no competitor can answer**: Phase 4 established KMV Projects built AIIMS Mangalagiri, which is this segment's workplace and sits inside the recommended micro-location. Smaller than S1 and more cash-constrained despite strong income — **which makes payment structuring, not price, the lever here.**

**S3 — US and developed-market Telugu diaspora.** *Score 4.20 — the highest — mid horizon.*
Clears Community D on income without strain and buys the highest-margin product. The pool is large; the *active* slice is small because 60%+ of NRI buyers currently look at Hyderabad, NCR or Bengaluru. **That is what makes it high priority rather than easy priority: the demand has to be created, and Phase 3 found no competitor doing the work properly.** Two hard operational constraints: the buying window is the December–January and summer India visit, and the relative who inspects on their behalf holds effective veto.

#### SECONDARY

**S4 — Amaravati land-pooling landowners.** *Score 4.05 — long horizon. Secondary on timing, not on importance.*
This is the most defensible white space in the entire assignment: ~25,000 households, ₹2.3–7.0 crore of land each, no competitor structured to serve them, and a KMV `/land-owners` proposition already published. It is placed **secondary only because the revenue arrives late and partly in kind** — an exchange is not a cash sale. **Reasoned: its true value is not its unit count. It supplies land, absorbs inventory, and creates local advocacy in the one community whose opinion the rest of the corridor watches.** Starting it in year one and expecting it in year three is the correct posture.

**S7 — Hyderabad-resident Telugu households with AP roots.** *Score 3.90, mid horizon.*
Diaspora capacity and emotional pull without the diaspora's verification problem, time-zone friction or once-a-year window — they can drive down on a Saturday. Lowest competitive intensity of any sizeable segment, because Phase 3 found the competitive set marketing almost entirely inside the local catchment. **Secondary rather than high for one honest reason: nothing forces the purchase.** No lease expiry, no commute, no school deadline. The absence of urgency is a real commercial problem and it caps how fast this segment can be converted regardless of budget.

**S6 — Vijayawada salaried professionals and upgraders.** *Score 3.20, near horizon.*
The largest and most reachable local pool, and it has to be worked because it fills Communities A and B. But it is **secondary on purpose**: Phase 3 counted ~3,269 competing units aimed at broadly this household, an 8:1 apartment-to-villa ratio. **Attacking it head-on is a price war, and the project's price architecture — ₹5,000–5,400/sq ft against Manjeera at ₹5,100 — has no room for one.** Work it with a specific non-price reason, not with volume media.

**S5 — Government and the relocating capital workforce.** *Score 2.95, near horizon.*
Placed here on a timing argument rather than a capacity one. On score alone it is weak: it does not clear the cash gate easily and it buys only Communities A and B. **But it holds the one genuinely dated liquidity event in this document.** The 8th CPC is effective 1 January 2026 with a 30–34% expected hike and arrears extending into 2027; AP's 9th PRC is constituted. **Retrospective arrears are precisely the ₹17.6–31.5 lakh lump sum the cash gate demands.** A segment that is ordinary in general can be the best available segment in a specific quarter. It also carries the largest sizing uncertainty here — no credible headcount for the relocating workforce was found (§7).

#### LOW PRIORITY

**S8 — Gulf remittance households.** *Score 2.45, long horizon.*
Real, and large by headcount, but its ticket capacity sits at Communities A and B — the crowded end — while requiring the full diaspora cost structure of Telugu-language video, WhatsApp operations, dual-market selling to both the earner abroad and the family at home, and a 9–24 month accumulation cycle. **High cost to serve, low revenue per unit.** Worth servicing when it arrives; not worth leading with.

**Investors.** *Not scored — deliberately.*
Phase 2 §9 found yields of **2.2–3.2%** including a **yield inversion in Tadepalli at 2.2%**; Phase 2 §10 found resale is not liquid; and the local investor's default asset is land, which appreciates without maintenance. **Beyond a modest share, investor buyers build a resale book that competes with the developer's own unsold inventory at handover.** This is a segment to cap, not to court.

**Private-university faculty and staff.** *Not a buyer segment.*
§2.1 and §4.1: pay averaging ~₹57,000/month sits below the Community A threshold. A rental market, not a purchase market.

**Non-Telugu out-of-state buyers.** *No evidence, no mechanism.*
Nothing found to support meaningful demand, and no plausible route to it at this stage of the city's development.

**Three things this reconciliation shows.**

**1. The three HIGH segments deliver 53.8% of revenue from 43% of units.** The priority order and the revenue architecture agree. That is the check passing.

**2. The most contested segment delivers the least revenue per unit.** S6 is 21% of units and 13.7% of revenue, at **₹0.83 crore per unit** against S1's ₹1.74 crore and S3's ₹1.96 crore. **Every marketing rupee spent winning a contested S6 sale buys less than half the revenue of an uncontested S3 sale.** That is the arithmetic case for the secondary classification, independent of any judgement about crowding.

**3. A correction to carry forward — the sales task is ~397 units, not ~600.**

Phase 1 §6.4 and §13.5 both state a sales task of **"approximately 600 units"** and an allowable marketing cost of **₹3.3–4.2 lakh per unit**. That estimate was made before Phase 2 §12 set the product mix, and it implied an average unit of ~1,389 sq ft across the whole scheme. The Phase 2 mix — 30% of area in 2,800–3,600 sq ft villas and 25% in 2,300–2,800 sq ft apartments — gives an average of **~2,098 sq ft** and therefore **~397 units**, not 600.

| | Phase 1 §6.4 estimate | Corrected on the Phase 2 §12 mix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Units | ~600 | **~397** |
| Avg unit size | ~1,389 sq ft | **~2,098 sq ft** |
| Bookings/month over 36 months | — | **11.0** |
| Bookings/month over 48 months | 10–12 | **8.3** |
| Allowable marketing cost per unit at ₹20–25 Cr | ₹3.3–4.2 L | **₹5.0–6.3 L** |

The absorption task is therefore **easier than Phase 1 concluded** — 8–11 bookings a month rather than 10–12 — and the **allowable cost per sale is roughly 50% higher**. That is comfortable against HIGH-segment ticket sizes and tight against the S6 and S8 bands, which is a further argument for weighting effort toward revenue per unit rather than headcount. **Phase 1 §6.4, §9 and §13 should be read with this correction applied.**

## 6. The community mapping — derived, not assumed

The brief was explicit that the audience for Communities A–D must not be decided in advance. It has not been. §1 established the financial gate, §2 the income sources, §3 the eight real segments, §4 the test against the brief's hypotheses, §5 the priority order. **Only now does the mapping fall out — and it falls out differently from how it would have been guessed.**

### 6.1 The mapping

| | **Community A** | **Community B** | **Community C** | **Community D** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Product** | 2BHK + compact 3BHK, 1,150–1,400 sq ft | 3BHK premium, 1,650–1,950 sq ft | 3.5/4BHK premium apartments, 2,300–2,800 sq ft | Villas, 2,800–3,600 sq ft |

### 6.2 Four things the derivation produced that assumption would not have

**1. Community D's largest buyer group will not live in it full time.**

**S3 (US diaspora, 25%) and S7 (Hyderabad, 20%) together take 45% of Community D — 35 units and ₹80.1 crore.** Both are absentee owners. Add the S4 parent generation, who *will* live there permanently, and Community D splits into two occupancy models inside one product.

> **Reasoned — the consequence is operational, not creative.** A villa community where nearly half the owners are absent needs **managed maintenance, remote handover, security designed for empty homes, and an optional rental-management service** as part of the product — not as an afterthought. Phase 3 found no competitor in the screened set offering this. **It is a differentiator that costs an operating model rather than a marketing budget**, which is why it is defensible.

**2. Community B is the largest unit count and sits in the most contested band.**

139 units — 35% of the scheme by count — at ₹5,400/sq ft, against a Phase 3 competitive set of roughly 3,269 mid-market apartment units. **This is the project's largest volume risk, and it was not visible until the audience work was done.** Community D is the revenue engine and has the thinnest competition; Community B is the volume engine and has the thickest. The marketing effort those two require is not proportional to their revenue.

**3. Segment 1 is not "the villa audience" — it spans the top three communities.**

S1's 70 units distribute as **B 14 · C 29 · D 27**. Trade and business families buy across the entire top half of the scheme, frequently as *multiple related households in the same project* (§3 S1). **That changes the sales approach from unit-selling to family-selling**, and it is why the seeding point in §5.4 matters more than any single campaign.

**4. Segment 2 is the only segment present in all four communities — because one institution's pay ladder maps onto the entire product ladder.**

| AIIMS Mangalagiri rung (§2.1) | Reaches | S2 units |
| --- | --- | --: |
| Senior Resident, ₹11–13 L p.a. | **Community A** | ~10 |
| Assistant Professor, ₹24–26 L | **Community B** | ~28 |
| Additional Professor, ₹36–52 L | **Community C** | ~21 |
| Professor, super-specialty, ₹36–48 L | **Community D** | ~6 |
| | | **~64** |

> **Established — and this is the most useful structural finding in the document.** The four rungs of one institution's published pay scale map cleanly onto the four communities. **A single institutional relationship can feed the entire product ladder** — a career-stage progression inside one workplace three kilometres from the site, built by the developer's own group company (Phase 4). No other segment has this property, and no competitor found in Phase 3 is positioned to claim it.

### 6.3 What this mapping does *not* settle

**It does not set the messaging, the channel plan or the media weight.** Those follow from it, and belong to the phases after this one.

**It does not resolve the Community D pricing question.** Phase 2 §12 flagged that Community D at ₹2.00–2.57 crore lands in the gap between the mid-premium villa cluster (topping out ~₹2.2 crore) and the luxury cluster (starting ~₹2.79 crore), and that the evidence base could not tell whether that is the project's best opportunity or its biggest pricing risk. **This document narrows it but does not close it.** What it adds: the segments that clear ₹2.28 crore on cash — S1, S3, S7 — are all real and all present in the catchment, which argues the gap is occupiable. What it cannot supply is proof that they will pay that specific number for this specific product.

**It does not verify the segment split within each community.** The percentages in §5.5 are a Reasoned planning assumption. **They should be replaced by actual booking data within the first two quarters of sales**, and the whole allocation re-run.

## 8. Carry-forward

**Established.**
- The binding constraint is **cash at purchase**, not income: ₹17.6 L for Community A rising to ₹74.1 L for Community D. It discriminates by **income source**, not income amount — which is why the segmentation is built on source.
- Community D requires household income around **10× district per capita GDP**. It cannot be sold primarily to salaried locals.
- **AIIMS is the corridor's high-income institution; the private universities are not.** SRM Assistant Professor pay (~₹6.8 L p.a.) sits below Community A.
- **The AIIMS pay ladder maps rung-for-rung onto Communities A–D** — one institutional relationship reaches the entire product ladder.
- **"NRI" is two segments.** US/developed-market households buy Communities C and D; Gulf households buy A and B. One campaign cannot serve both.
- **"HNI" is not a segment here, and Community D at ₹2.57 crore is not an HNI product.**
- **~25,000 land-pooling households** hold ₹2.3–7.0 crore of land each against annuity income under ₹2 lakh — land-rich, cash-thin, and unreachable by any conventional sales structure.

**Corrections to earlier phases.**
- **The sales task is ~397 units, not ~600** (Phase 1 §6.4, §9, §13). Average unit size rises from ~1,389 to ~2,098 sq ft on the Phase 2 §12 mix. Bookings needed fall to **8.3–11.0 a month**; allowable marketing cost per unit rises to **₹5.0–6.3 lakh**.

**Reasoned, and carried forward as working assumptions.**
- Priority order: **HIGH — S1 trade families, S2 senior medical professionals, S3 US diaspora.** **SECONDARY — S4 landowners, S7 Hyderabad, S6 upgraders, S5 government.** **LOW — S8 Gulf, investors.**
- The three HIGH segments deliver **53.8% of revenue from 43% of units**.
- **45% of Community D revenue comes from buyers who will not live there full time** — which makes managed maintenance and absentee ownership a product requirement, not an amenity.
- **Community B is the largest volume risk**: 139 units, 35% of the scheme, in the band where Phase 3 counted ~3,269 competing units.
- Two dated opportunities exist and both are known now: the **pay-revision arrears window** for S5, and the **December–January and summer India-visit windows** for S3.

**Open.** The fifteen gaps in §7 — of which the six sizing gaps are closeable from registration and institutional data before any budget is committed.
