The brief's constraint

Investment model · bottom-up build · allocation and returns

Built from thirteen line items upward, not from a percentage downward — and the two answers disagree by 28%

§0

Method

Phases 7 and 9 produced this budget top-down. This one builds it from the other end and lets the two disagree.

The headline result

₹32.99 crore bottom-up against ₹25.68 crore top-down. A 28% gap.

That gap is not a rounding error. It sits almost entirely in the things that do not look like media — launch, production, technology, NRI infrastructure, events, and the agency fee nobody was paying. §2 maps every Phase 9 rupee onto the thirteen cost lines, so the two columns reconcile rather than merely differ.

What top-down did
  • Phase 7 assumed a brand line and a sales-infrastructure line — both tagged ASSUMPTION — and derived lead generation from CPL × leads.
  • Phase 9 distributed the resulting total across 22 channels.
  • Internally consistent. It just never priced anything.
What this phase does
  • Every line is quantity × unit cost.
  • Sourced where a published Indian rate exists; labelled where it does not.
  • Summed first, compared second — so the comparison is a finding, not a target.
The four classifications, carried from Phase 7
  • Market data observed in this catchment during Phases 1–6.
  • Benchmark a published Indian rate. Most come from agencies and vendors — interested parties — and are treated as directional.
  • Assumption set here, reasoning shown. Observed nowhere.
  • Calculated derived arithmetically from the above.
§0.3 · a taxonomy note the brief's list requires

The brief's twelve items are a cost taxonomy. Phase 9's four layers are an accountability taxonomy. Both are valid; they are different cuts of the same money, and they overlap. Five collisions are resolved explicitly rather than left to happen:

OverlapHow it is resolved here
Outdoor sits inside brand buildingOutdoor is broken out as its own line because it is 6.9% of the budget and has its own published rate card. Brand building excludes it.
Events overlap sales enablementOn-site owner events sit in Events. The experience centre and show homes sit in Sales enablement.
NRI acquisition is an audience, not a channel — Phase 9 said soIt survives as a budget line, and should. The media for S3 and S8 sits inside Performance marketing; this line holds only the incremental infrastructure that audience needs and no other does. §1.11 states the exclusion explicitly, because double-counting here is the obvious trap.
CRM and Technology are adjacentCRM holds licences, integration and telephony. Technology holds the website, martech, analytics and the walkthrough platform.
The referral programme has no slot in the brief's twelvePhase 9 funded referral rewards and administration at ₹0.70 Cr and modelled 70 bookings from them. Referral is neither a channel line nor a cost type the twelve names cover, so it is carried as an explicit thirteenth line rather than hidden inside Events. §2 shows what happens when it is dropped: the reconciliation stops closing.

The pattern, stated plainly

A top-down percentage systematically under-funds everything that is not media — the production that fills the media, the technology that captures its output, the people who run it, and the infrastructure a specific audience needs.

It over-funds the one thing that looks expensive and is partly recoverable. That is the case against "spend 10% of revenue" in a single table.

And one finding that came out of building the table rather than reading it

A first version of this reconciliation summed to ₹4.61 crore against a ₹6.61 crore gap — ₹2.00 crore adrift. Two lines had been eyeballed against Phase 9 rather than mapped to it, and the referral programme had been dropped from the bottom-up build entirely while the CAC table continued to charge its ₹0.70 crore against 70 referral bookings.

Forcing the reconciliation to close is what found it. A decomposition that does not sum to the difference it claims to explain is not a decomposition.

This is the argument for building the table both ways round — and a reason to distrust any budget variance analysis whose rows do not add to the variance.

§5.1 · what the ₹1.24 crore trim actually removes

RECOMMENDED trims ₹1.24 crore from things that do not touch performance media — Telugu OTT from 24 to 18 months, cinema from 12 to 6, the hoarding sequence from 24 to 20 months, standing boards from 30 to 24, one diaspora event deferred, four owner films fewer, and the PR retainer from 30 to 24 months.

It delivers the same 376 bookings for ₹1.24 crore less, because none of those cuts touches the funnel. CONSTRAINED does touch it, and §4 is what that costs.

§5.2 · On being above the 4–5% industry norm

Phase 7 anchored against an industry-normal marketing spend of 4–5% of GDV. The recommendation here is 6.27%. That difference deserves an answer rather than a footnote.

1 · No existing brand to spend against
  • Phase 4 found thin search visibility and sparse reviews.
  • The 4–5% norm largely describes developers with established recall and a working referral base.
2 · No referral base in year one
  • 18.5% of bookings are modelled as referral-sourced, and referral requires prior buyers.
  • In year one there are none, so paid channels carry more than the steady-state average implies.
3 · Deliberate over-investment in sales enablement
  • ₹3.78 crore on the experience centre, show homes and collateral.
  • Because Phase 8 valued that stage at ₹39.4 crore per conversion point. A considered allocation, not overspend.
4 · Two of eight segments are overseas
  • Community B alone consumes 65% of the lead-generation budget.
  • Overseas acquisition is structurally more expensive than domestic, and this project has more of it than a typical corridor scheme.
Reasoned

6.27% is defensible for a first project, by a developer without established consumer recall, selling 31% of its revenue in an unproven price band.

It should fall toward the norm from year three onward, as referral compounds and brand recall accumulates — and the model should be re-based annually rather than fixed at launch.

§6.1 · the working-capital finding

Peak monthly burn is pre-launch, at ₹1.15 crore a month — 2.05× the post-launch run-rate — and it lands when revenue is exactly zero.

₹6.89 crore is committed before the first booking. Of that, ₹3.55 crore is sales enablement, technology and CRM: capital that must exist for the funnel to function at all. The experience centre cannot be half-built and the CRM cannot be half-integrated.

Reasoned — the operational consequence. This is a cash-flow item, not a marketing item, and it belongs in the project's funding plan rather than in a monthly marketing report. A marketing budget presented as a flat monthly average conceals it entirely — which is another argument against the percentage-of-revenue approach, since a percentage of zero revenue is zero.

§7

Channel allocation

The same ₹31.75 crore, cut by channel rather than by cost type. The launch line is decomposed into its channel components so nothing is hidden.

1 · Paid media is 46% of the budget
  • ₹14.65 crore of media against ₹17.10 crore of production, capital, technology, people and commission.
  • A budget conversation conducted entirely in media terms is discussing less than half the money.
2 · What that means for the review cadence
  • A weekly performance review covers 46% of the spend and produces most of the reporting.
  • The other 54% is reviewed quarterly at best, and it contains every line that must be right before the media runs.
  • Phase 9's rule stands: compare within a layer, never across.
§8

Community allocation

Directly attributable costs traced; everything shared allocated by revenue share — a choice, and stated as one.

Method, stated because it is a choice

Costs split into directly attributable — performance media by community from Phase 7, the agency fee pro-rata, and the NRI infrastructure which serves Community B alone — and shared, which is everything else and is allocated by revenue share.

Reasoned. Revenue share is the defensible basis here because the brand, the site, the technology and the launch serve all four communities and cannot be traced to any one. Allocating by unit count instead would load Community A with 45% of the shared cost for 25% of the revenue, which would misrepresent it.

The finding this restates at full-budget scale

Community B consumes 40% of the total budget for 22% of the units and 22% of the revenue — and its acquisition cost is 11.23% of its own revenue, against 4.5–4.6% for A and C.

It is expensive because it is building a capability that does not exist, not because the media is being wasted. The two call for opposite responses, and telling them apart is the whole point of splitting direct from shared.

Five findings a percentage-of-revenue rule would have concealed

1 · Bottom-up exceeds top-down by 28%
  • And the gap sits entirely in things that are not media: the launch spread invisibly, the agency fee nobody budgeted, the NRI infrastructure with no home, the technology build with no line, the production that fills the media.
2 · 54% of the budget is not media
  • A budget conversation held in media terms is discussing less than half the money — and the half it skips contains every line that must be right before the media runs.
3 · Only ₹21.17 crore is genuinely flexible
  • ₹7.24 crore is committed before revenue exists.
  • ₹3.34 crore costs nothing until a unit sells.
  • Negotiating over ₹31.75 crore is negotiating over the wrong number.
4 · Every ₹1 cut from performance media risks ₹24.60
  • Cutting to Phase 9's ₹25.68 crore saves ₹6.50 crore and risks ₹74.2 crore.
  • That is the answer to "why not less?"
5 · Peak burn is pre-launch, at 2.05× the steady state
  • And it lands when revenue is zero.
  • A flat monthly average conceals it entirely — and a percentage of zero revenue is zero.
And one the method caught on itself
  • The first reconciliation did not sum to the gap it claimed to explain, which is how the dropped referral programme surfaced.
  • A variance analysis whose rows do not add to the variance is not evidence.
