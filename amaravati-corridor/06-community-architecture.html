§1

Testing the default assignment

A = 2BHK, B = 3BHK, C = premium apartments, D = villas. It was used in Phase 2 as a feasibility test, never validated as a customer architecture. Tested now, it fails three ways.

Failure 1 · Configuration is not a pricing lever here

Established The 2BHK gated band is ₹3,800–4,800/sq ft. The 3BHK mid-gated band is ₹3,800–4,600/sq ft. They overlap almost completely.

Phase 2 §4.1: in this corridor, moving from a 2BHK to a 3BHK buys more area at roughly the same rate — not a better rate. Size and address price. Bedroom count does not.

So A and B split by configuration sit at the same ₹/sq ft, in the same building type, for the same buyer, on the same land. That is one community with two floor plans — and calling it two costs two brand identities, two show homes and two sales narratives while buying nothing.

Failure 3 · the area goes where the competition is

Established Phase 3 counted roughly 3,269 mid-market apartment units against ~420 villa units — 8:1. The crowding is at the apartment end.

Computed In the default structure A, B and C are all apartments: 45% of land and 70% of area, with B and C alone carrying 55% of area inside or adjacent to Prime Grandeur's range.

Phase 5 §6.2 reached the same conclusion from the audience side — Community B was the largest unit count in the scheme, in the most contested band. Two independent analyses, same answer.

What survives. Two of the four assignments hold. A as the entry apartment tier — the brief fixes the spectrum's floor at 2BHK apartments. And D as villas, which strengthens: Phase 2 found KMV Vivaan achieved ₹8,214–8,271/sq ft, 30% above the best apartment rate recorded anywhere in the region. Villas are the only product type in the evidence base that reliably clears ₹7,000/sq ft. What fails is the middle.

The reframe

Established ₹500 crore is not the capacity of 25 acres. It is a low-density choice — roughly 60% of what the land would yield at the lowest apartment density observed anywhere nearby.

This resolves a tension running through the whole exercise. Phase 1 §6.4 framed the central problem as "manufacture and defend a price premium of roughly 45%." Phase 2 §11 softened it — against comparable product the premium is +16% over Manjeera and −6% versus Vertex. Phase 6 closes it:

The premium is not manufactured by marketing. It is purchased with density — at a price of roughly ₹350–500 crore in forgone gross development value.

The operational consequence. Density is not a residual output of the product mix. It is the product. Any later proposal to "add a tower" or "improve yield" is not a yield decision — it is a decision to spend the differentiator the price premium rests on, and it should face the same scrutiny as a proposal to cut price. Because arithmetically it is the same decision.

§5

Why A rather than B. Why C rather than D.

Two questions the brief asks directly. The second has the sharper answer.

Why would a customer choose A rather than B?

For most buyers this is not a choice — and that is the point. A tops out at ₹90 lakh; B starts at ₹1.23 crore. The gap is ₹33 lakh, or 36%. Phase 5's financial gate does the separating: an A buyer needs ₹17.6–24 lakh in cash, a B buyer needs ₹40 lakh. Very few households sit on both sides of that line.

The narrow band that does face the choice is a household at ₹1.0–1.2 crore of capacity — a dual-income S6 upgrader, or an S2 assistant professor with a working spouse.

Choose A if

Occupancy — you will live here daily.

Household — young children who need ground-level play space.

What you get — more home for the money, at half the corridor's density.

The trade — you handle your own maintenance.

Choose B if

Occupancy — you will be away for long stretches.

Household — no children at home, or ageing parents needing single-level living.

What you get — less home, plus an operating service that keeps it intact when you are not in it.

The trade — you pay for management you would not need if you were here.

The separation is occupancy, not aspiration

Reasoned A is not "the cheap one" and B is not "the better one." B is worse value per square foot for a resident family and better value for an absent owner.

Selling B to a resident family is how this architecture would fail — that buyer discovers within a year that they paid for a service they do not use.

The cleanest answer in this architecture

Established D does not sell more house. It sells more land.

The built-up difference is about 25%. The plot difference is about 50%. Roughly two-thirds of the price step is land, not construction.

Why that works on this audience. Phase 5 found the primary audience for both C and D — Vijayawada and Guntur trade families — has as its rational driver "land appreciation logic applied to built product", and as its default alternative "why not just buy land?" D answers that objection inside the project rather than losing to it outside. A segment that already thinks in square yards is being sold square yards, in the one form that also comes with a house on it.

And the guard against the reverse. A C buyer is not a failed D buyer. C is the right product for a nuclear family that wants ground without maintaining 350 square yards of it — Phase 5's senior faculty and dual-doctor households, whose scarcest resource is time.

§6

How the four avoid cannibalising each other

Cannibalisation happens when two products compete for the same buyer's single purchase. Five mechanisms, in descending order of how much work each does.

Pricing rule 1 No Community B unit is ever priced above ₹1.50 crore — whatever the floor premium or the view.
Pricing rule 2 No Community C villa is ever discounted below ₹1.65 crore — whatever the quarter-end pressure.

Breaking either rule closes the gap and turns a ₹24 lakh separation into a direct comparison the apartment loses.

Mechanism 2 · a binary attribute at every join, not a gradient

A buyer can argue about ₹24 lakh. They cannot argue about whether a home is detached.

JoinThe binary
A → BManaged / not managed
B → CDetached / not detached
C → DLarge plot / compact plot — fixed at the masterplan stage, so it cannot drift

Mechanisms 3 and 4 · different occupancy, different calendars

B is the only community designed to be empty — that is not a feature comparison, it is a different use of a home. And Phase 5 established the triggers and channels independently; they barely overlap.

Purchase triggerChannel
APay-revision arrears; rent revisionPortals, search, departmental word-of-mouth
BThe December–January or summer India visitDiaspora digital, APNRTS, community associations
CA trading season; a peer buyingReferral, personal introduction, site visit
DA land sale; a marriage; a peer buyingReferral, seeded; village relations for S4

Two communities marketed on different calendars, in different languages, to different countries, are not competing for the same buyer.

Mechanism 5 · the risk nobody names

Reasoned The real cannibalisation risk here is not between communities. It is between tranches.

Four communities × three to four tranches is up to fifteen inventory releases. Two open at once in adjacent bands means the sales team is arbitraging its own price list.

The rule: never hold open inventory in two adjacent communities at the same time. A and C can be open together — ₹59 lakh and ₹1.7 crore do not compete. B and C must not be.

§7

What makes this one development rather than four projects

Distinct propositions create a risk the brief implies but does not state — four communities that share nothing forfeit the scale advantage the price premium rests on.

Bound by a shared spine
  • One gate, one address
  • A central open space every community fronts onto rather than backs away from
  • One main clubhouse, one retail row, one school site, one sports zone
  • The 25 acres must be legible as 25 acres from inside any of the four — that is what a 5-to-11-acre competitor cannot answer, and it only works if the open space is central and shared rather than divided four ways
Bound by one operator
  • Security, STP, water, power backup, waste, landscaping — one system, one facilities operator, one standard
  • This is also what makes Community B's managed service economically possible: an increment on an operator that already exists, not a standalone business
Bound by an upgrade path
  • A documented internal move-up right from A into C or D in a later phase — priority allocation plus a transfer mechanism
  • Phase 5 found upgraders are a real segment. The cheapest upgrader to acquire is one who already lives inside the gate.
The one thing that must not be shared

Not a single amenity charge. This is the failure mode to avoid.

If all four communities pay into one maintenance pool, D's 63 households subsidise A's 169 — and D buyers, a segment Phase 5 found highly attentive to who else is buying, will notice.

Three layers, priced separately: a shared-spine charge everyone pays per square foot · community-level charges for community-level amenities · an opt-in managed-service charge that exists only in B.

The concentration, stated plainly
