# Phase 6 — Community Architecture

## How 25 acres divides into four communities, tested rather than assumed

**Prepared:** August 2026
**Purpose:** Product and land structure for the hypothetical 25-acre, ₹500 crore corridor development
**Method note:** The brief is explicit — *do not automatically assign A = 2BHK, B = 3BHK, C = premium, D = villas unless research supports that structure.* §1 tests that assignment against the Phase 2–5 evidence and finds three reasons it fails. §2–§3 establish what actually differentiates in this market. §4 builds the alternative.

---

## 0. What this phase does

Phases 1–5 produced an evidence base, a price ladder, a competitor field, a brand baseline and a buyer universe. This phase turns them into **a physical and commercial structure**: how much land goes where, what gets built on it, at what price, for whom.

Three things make that a real analytical problem rather than a labelling exercise:

1. **The intuitive assignment is available and wrong in specific, checkable ways.** §1.
2. **The land can support far more than ₹500 crore.** That changes what the ₹500 crore figure means. §2.
3. **The obvious differentiator — bedroom count — does not price in this market.** Phase 2 established it and it invalidates the usual A/B split. §3.

Everything below is labelled **Established** (from named Phase 1–5 evidence), **Computed** (arithmetic shown), or **Reasoned** (judgement, testable, flagged in §8).

---

## 1. Testing the default assignment

The default — A = 2BHK, B = 3BHK, C = premium apartments, D = villas — is the structure Phase 2 §12 used as a *feasibility test*. It was never validated as a customer architecture. Tested now, it fails on three counts.

### 1.1 Configuration is not a pricing lever in this corridor

**Established — Phase 2 §4.1.** The 2BHK gated band is **₹3,800–4,800/sq ft**. The 3BHK mid-gated band is **₹3,800–4,600/sq ft**. They overlap almost completely.

> In this corridor, moving from a 2BHK to a 3BHK buys **more area at roughly the same rate** — not a better rate. Configuration is not a pricing lever. **Size and address are.**

**The consequence for community structure.** If A and B differ only by bedroom count, they sit at effectively the same ₹/sq ft, in the same building type, for the same buyer, on the same land. That is not two communities with distinct propositions. **It is one community with two floor plans**, and calling it two communities buys nothing while costing two brand identities, two show homes and two sales narratives.

### 1.2 Community C — premium apartments — is priced into a fight it loses

This is the most serious failure, and it carries the second-largest revenue block in the default structure.

**Established — Phase 2 §3.** **Prime Grandeur, Mangalagiri, 10 acres, 119 detached villas, 2,480–3,374 sq ft, ₹1.34–1.82 crore, derived rate ₹5,394–5,403/sq ft** (both ends of the published range agree to within 0.2%, so this is high-confidence evidence, not a listing artefact).

**The default Community C** is premium apartments, 2,300–2,800 sq ft, **₹1.44–1.75 crore at ₹6,250/sq ft**.

| | Default Community C | Prime Grandeur |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Form | Apartment | **Detached villa** |
| Size | 2,300–2,800 sq ft | 2,480–3,374 sq ft |
| Ticket | ₹1.44–1.75 Cr | **₹1.34–1.82 Cr** |
| Rate | **₹6,250/sq ft** | **₹5,400/sq ft** |
| Micro-market | Mangalagiri corridor | **Mangalagiri** |

> **Established. In the same micro-market, at the same ticket, for a similar size, the competing product is a detached villa at a rate 16% lower.** A premium apartment at ₹1.44–1.75 crore is asking a buyer to pay more per square foot for less form, in a market where the villa alternative is already built and RERA-registered.

There is a second problem stacked on the first. **Phase 2 §4.1 established that the corridor's apartment ceiling of ₹6,300–6,500/sq ft is a Vijayawada city-centre phenomenon** — set by Vertex Siris Signa, a 3-acre, 184-unit ready scheme inside the city. **Nothing found in Mangalagiri or Tadepalli reaches it.** So the default Community C requires importing a city-centre rate into a corridor *and* winning against a villa at that ticket. Phase 2 already flagged this as "the central pricing risk for the hypothetical project's premium apartment communities." Phase 6 concludes it is not a risk to manage but a structure to change.

### 1.3 The default puts the most area in the most contested band

**Established — Phase 3 §4.** The competitive set holds roughly **3,269 mid-market apartment units against ~420 villa units — an 8:1 ratio.** The crowding is at the apartment end.

**Computed.** In the default structure, Communities A, B and C are all apartments: **45% of land and 70% of area**, of which B and C alone carry 55% of area, sitting in or adjacent to Prime Grandeur's ₹1.34–1.82 crore range.

**Phase 5 §6.2 found the same thing from the audience side** — Community B at 139 units was the largest unit count in the scheme, in the most contested band. Two independent analyses reach the same conclusion.

### 1.4 What survives the test

The default is not wholly wrong. **Two of its four assignments hold:**

- **A as the entry apartment tier holds** — the brief fixes the spectrum's floor at 2BHK apartments, and the evidence supports an apartment product there.
- **D as villas holds, and strengthens.** Phase 2 §4.1: villas achieved ₹8,214–8,271/sq ft (KMV Vivaan) — **30% above the best apartment rate found anywhere in the region.** Villas are the only product type in the evidence base that reliably clears ₹7,000/sq ft.

**What fails is the middle.** B and C as two apartment tiers separated by bedroom count, priced into the corridor's most contested and most villa-exposed territory.

## 2. The finding that reframes the whole exercise

Before restructuring, one piece of arithmetic has to be surfaced, because it was implicit in Phases 1–2 and never stated.

### 2.1 What density did the earlier phases assume?

**Computed** — from Phase 2 §12's area shares applied to 25 acres:

| Community | Area share | Acres | Saleable | Units | **Units/acre** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | 15% | 3.75 | 1.25 L sq ft | 98 | 26.1 |
| B | 30% | 7.50 | 2.50 L sq ft | 139 | 18.5 |
| C | 25% | 6.25 | 2.08 L sq ft | 82 | 13.1 |
| D | 30% | 7.50 | 2.50 L sq ft | 78 | 10.4 |
| **Apartments (A+B+C)** | 70% | **17.50** | **5.83 L sq ft** | **318** | **18.2** |

**Established — Phase 2 §5.** Corridor apartment schemes run at **52.9 to 113.4 units per acre** (Jayabheri 52.9, Vertex 61.3, Samruddhi 62.5, Aparna 65.9, Sree Amaravati Icon 78.8, Raintree Park II 111.5, Manjeera Monarch 113.4).

> **The Phase 2 mix silently assumed an apartment density 2.9× to 6.2× lower than any apartment scheme in the corridor.** At 18.2 units per acre the "apartments" are not apartments as this market builds them. That was never stated as a decision.

### 2.2 So what could the land actually produce?

**Computed** — 25 acres at 60% apartments / 40% villas, villas held at 8 units/acre, apartments at observed corridor densities:

| Apartment density | Anchor | Apartments | Villas | Saleable | **Indicative GDV** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 52.9 / acre | Jayabheri — the corridor's **lowest** | 794 | 80 | 15.4 L sq ft | **~₹851 Cr** |
| 65.9 / acre | Aparna Amaravati One | 989 | 80 | 18.5 L sq ft | **~₹1,013 Cr** |
| 113.4 / acre | Manjeera Monarch | 1,701 | 80 | 29.9 L sq ft | **~₹1,606 Cr** |

*(Indicative only: apartments at ₹5,200/sq ft, villas at ₹7,000/sq ft, average apartment 1,600 sq ft and villa 3,400 sq ft.)*

### 2.3 The reframe

> **Established. ₹500 crore is not the capacity of 25 acres in this corridor. It is roughly 60% of what the land would yield at the lowest apartment density observed anywhere nearby, and about a third of what it would yield at the corridor's mid density.**
>
> **₹500 crore on 25 acres is therefore not a stretch target. It is a low-density choice** — the project deliberately forgoing something in the order of ₹350–500 crore of potential gross development value.

This resolves a tension that has run through the whole exercise. **Phase 1 §6.4** framed the central problem as *"manufacture and defend a price premium of roughly 45% over the surrounding market."* **Phase 2 §11** softened it — against genuinely comparable product the premium is +16% over Manjeera Monarch and −6% versus Vertex Siris Signa, not 45%. **Phase 6 closes it:**

**The premium is not manufactured by marketing. It is purchased with density.** Phase 2 §5.1 already identified this as the strongest differentiator available — *"a buyer comparing a ₹1.5 crore 3BHK here against Manjeera Monarch is choosing between roughly 20 homes per acre and 113. That is a difference visible from the site gate."* What Phase 6 adds is the price of that differentiator, in rupees of forgone GDV, and the conclusion that **it is worth paying and must be protected.**

**The operational consequence.** Density is not a residual output of the product mix. It is the product. Any later decision to "add a tower" or "improve yield" is not a yield decision — it is a decision to spend the differentiator that the price premium rests on. That should require the same scrutiny as a decision to cut price, because it is the same decision.

## 3. What actually differentiates in this market

If bedroom count does not price (§1.1), what does? Three candidate levers, tested against the Phase 2 evidence.

| Candidate lever | Does it price? | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Configuration** (2BHK vs 3BHK) | **No** | Phase 2 §4.1 — the bands overlap almost completely |
| **Built form** (apartment / attached / detached villa) | **Yes, strongly** | Villas clear ₹7,300–8,300/sq ft; apartments cap at ₹6,300–6,500. **A ~30% step** |
| **Occupancy model** (resident vs absentee) | **Not on rate — on proposition** | Phase 5 §6.2: 45% of top-end revenue is absentee, with a different requirement set |

**Established — built form is the only in-project rate lever the evidence supports**, and it is a large one. **Reasoned — occupancy model is the only in-project *proposition* lever that creates a distinct community without needing a price step**, which is what makes it useful where the price ladder runs out.

### 3.1 The price map this produces

**Established — Phase 2 §3.1 and §4.** Laying the corridor's actual price territory on one line:

| Territory | Rate | Ticket | Occupancy |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Crowded apartment band | ₹3,800–5,400 | ₹45 L – 1.05 Cr | ~3,269 competing units (Phase 3) |
| **Prime Grandeur's ground** | **₹5,400** | **₹1.34 – 1.82 Cr** | **Detached villas at apartment-tier rates** |
| Corridor apartment ceiling | ₹6,300–6,500 | to ~₹1.95 Cr | City-centre only (Vertex) |
| **The empty band** | **₹5,900 – 7,300** | **₹2.2 – 2.8 Cr** | **"Almost nothing between." The least contested territory found anywhere in this research** |
| Luxury villas | ₹7,300–8,300 | ₹2.8 – 3.8 Cr | TAG AIRA ₹7,309; KMV Vivaan ₹8,214–8,271 |

**Reasoned — this is a barbell market, not a ladder market.** The bottom is crowded, the ₹1.3–1.9 crore middle is defended by a detached-villa product at apartment pricing, and the ₹2.2–2.8 crore band is empty. **A structure that puts most of its area in the middle is putting it where the competition is strongest and the form disadvantage is worst.**

One caution carried forward from Phase 2 §3.1 and not resolved here: *whether the ₹2.2–2.8 crore band is empty because nobody has tried, or because buyers refuse it, cannot be determined from listing data alone.* §8 quantifies the exposure.

## 4. The recommended architecture

Four communities differentiated by **built form and ground relationship** — the lever §3 established actually prices — with **occupancy model** used to separate the two communities the price ladder cannot separate on its own.

### 4.1 The structure at a glance

| | **A** | **B** | **C** | **D** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Product type** | Low-rise garden apartments, 2BHK and 3BHK | Large managed residences — lift-served, single-level, lock-and-leave | Compact detached villas, G+1, ~200–225 sq yd plots | Estate villas, G+1/G+2, ~300–350 sq yd plots |
| **Unit size** | 1,150 – 1,750 sq ft | 2,150 – 2,550 sq ft | 2,600 – 3,000 sq ft | 3,200 – 3,800 sq ft |
| **Price band** | **₹5,150 / sq ft** | **₹5,700 / sq ft** | **₹6,500 / sq ft** | **₹7,200 / sq ft** |
| **Ticket size** | **₹59 – 90 L** | **₹1.23 – 1.45 Cr** | **₹1.69 – 1.95 Cr** | **₹2.30 – 2.74 Cr** |
| **Land allocation** | **6.5 ac** (26%) | **3.5 ac** (14%) | **6.0 ac** (24%) | **9.0 ac** (36%) |
| **Density** | 26 units/acre | 24 units/acre | 10 units/acre | 7 units/acre |
| **Units** | **169** (45%) | **84** (22%) | **60** (16%) | **63** (17%) |
| **Revenue** | **₹126.2 Cr** (24.9%) | **₹112.5 Cr** (22.2%) | **₹109.2 Cr** (21.6%) | **₹158.8 Cr** (31.3%) |

**Totals:** 25.0 acres · **376 units** · 8.31 lakh sq ft saleable · gross FAR 0.76 · **15.0 units per acre** · blended **₹6,098/sq ft** · average ticket ₹1.35 Cr · **₹506.7 crore**

**Computed — a convergence worth noting.** This structure was built from the land side: acres × density × unit size × rate. It arrives at **8.31 lakh sq ft** and **gross FAR 0.76**. Phase 1 §6.4, working from the revenue side, derived **8.33 lakh sq ft at FAR 0.77**. Two independent routes land within 0.3% of each other.

### 4.2 The land logic

| Allocation | Acres | Share | Rationale |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Apartment forms (A + B)** | 10.0 | 40% | Carries 253 of 376 units (67%) on 40% of land — the density is here because the buyers here are price-led |
| **Villa forms (C + D)** | 15.0 | 60% | Carries 53% of revenue on 33% of units — the margin is here, and villas are the only product clearing ₹7,000/sq ft |

**Reasoned.** The 60/40 land split toward villas inverts the default structure's 45/55 toward apartments. It follows directly from §1.3 (the apartment band is 8:1 oversupplied) and §3.1 (the empty band is at the top).

**Established — this remains internally consistent with the earlier phases.** Phase 1 §6.4 predicted *"a 50:50 to 60:40 land split between apartments and villas"* from the GDV arithmetic alone. Phase 2 §5.1 independently predicted a density of *"≈16–22 units/acre."* This structure lands at **60:40 and 15.0 units/acre** — at the edge of both predicted ranges, on the low-density side, deliberately.

### 4.3 Community A — The Apartments

*The entry to the address, and the scheme's cash-flow engine.*

| Attribute | |
| --- | --- |
| **Product type** | **Low-rise garden apartments**, G+3, 2BHK and 3BHK in one community rather than two. Lift-served but walk-up scale; no high-rise fire-lift or deep-foundation cost. |
| **Unit size** | 1,150 – 1,750 sq ft. 2BHK 1,150–1,350; 3BHK 1,500–1,750. |
| **Price band** | ₹5,150 / sq ft — the upper half of Phase 2's ₹4,500–5,400 premium-gated 3BHK band, and **above** the ₹3,800–4,800 gated-2BHK band. |
| **Ticket size** | **₹59 – 90 L** |
| **Land allocation** | **6.5 acres, 26% of site, 26 units/acre** |
| **Inventory logic** | 169 units, released in **three tranches of ~55**. First tranche at launch to establish price and generate velocity; second on foundation completion; third held to capture price appreciation. **The 2BHK share should be capped at roughly 40% of the community** — it is the most contested product in the corridor and the lowest revenue per unit in the scheme. |
| **Primary audience** | **S5 — Government and the relocating capital workforce** (Phase 5). The most loan-friendly income in the catchment; constrained by down payment, not EMI. |
| **Secondary audience** | S6 Vijayawada upgraders (younger cohort) · S8 Gulf remittance households · S2 senior residents at AIIMS |
| **Customer need** | Ending a long rental period. An owned, secure, gated home within reach of a workplace that is itself relocating. Efficient carpet area, school access, reliable water and power, covered parking. |
| **Purchase trigger** | **The pay-revision arrears window.** The 8th CPC is effective 1 January 2026 at an expected 30–34%, with arrears extending into 2027; AP's 9th PRC is constituted. Retrospective arrears are precisely the ₹17.6–24 lakh lump sum the down payment requires (Phase 5 §1.2). Secondary: rent revision, a child reaching school age, confirmation of posting. |
| **Positioning** | **The lowest-density apartment in the corridor.** At 26 units/acre against a corridor range of 52.9–113.4, this is **half the density of the least dense competitor**. That is not a brochure claim; it is countable from the site gate. |
| **Value proposition** | *A first home inside a 25-acre address, at half the density of anything else at this price.* The buyer is not paying a premium for the apartment — they are paying it for the master plan the apartment sits inside, and for an upgrade path within the same gate. |
| **Likely demand** | **Deep but contested.** Phase 3 counted ~3,269 competing mid-market units. Phase 5 sized S5 as unverified (no relocating-workforce headcount exists) and S6 as the largest local pool. **Demand is not the constraint here; differentiation is.** |
| **Revenue contribution** | **₹126.2 Cr — 24.9% of revenue from 44.9% of units.** Lowest revenue per unit in the scheme at ₹0.75 Cr. |
| **Strategic role** | **Four jobs.** (1) **Cash flow** — early, fast, loan-backed sales that fund construction. (2) **Brief compliance** — the spectrum's fixed floor. (3) **Population** — a 25-acre scheme needs residents for its amenities to feel alive at handover; 169 households do that faster than 63 villas. (4) **Upgrade feeder** — an A buyer in 2029 is a C buyer in 2036, inside the same brand. |

### 4.4 Community B — The Managed Residences

*The community that exists because 45% of premium demand will not be living here.*

| Attribute | |
| --- | --- |
| **Product type** | **Large single-level residences with a managed-ownership wrapper.** Lift-served, 3.5BHK and 4BHK, upper floors prioritised for outlook. The product is the **service**: building-managed maintenance, housekeeping on schedule, remote handover, security designed for homes that are empty, and an optional rental-management desk. |
| **Unit size** | 2,150 – 2,550 sq ft |
| **Price band** | **₹5,700 / sq ft** — deliberately **6% below Vertex Siris Signa's ₹6,300**, the corridor apartment ceiling, and **below** the level at which a detached villa becomes available. |
| **Ticket size** | **₹1.23 – 1.45 Cr** — set to top out **below Prime Grandeur's ₹1.82 Cr ceiling**, so this product is never the expensive way to buy less form. |
| **Land allocation** | **3.5 acres, 14% of site, 24 units/acre** |
| **Inventory logic** | 84 units. **The smallest community, deliberately** — it is the least proven proposition in the corridor and the most expensive audience to acquire (Phase 7 §6). Release in **two tranches of ~42**, the second only after the managed-service model has been demonstrated on the first. |
| **Primary audience** | **S3 — US and developed-market Telugu diaspora** (Phase 5, priority score 4.20, the highest). Clears this ticket without strain. |
| **Secondary audience** | S7 Hyderabad-resident Telugu households · **S4 land-pooling parent generation** (single-level living, lift access) · S2 senior AIIMS faculty · retiring buyers from any segment |
| **Customer need** | **Lock-and-leave.** Phase 5 §3 S3: the home will be empty eleven months a year, and the defining requirement is that it can be. Also: single-level living for ageing parents and owners; guest capacity for the annual visit; a home that does not deteriorate when nobody is in it. |
| **Purchase trigger** | **The India visit — December–January and summer**, and no other window (Phase 5 §3 S3). Secondary: a parent's health event, a vesting or bonus event, a sibling buying. For S4 and S7: a family visit, festival, or wedding that brings them into the catchment. |
| **Positioning** | **The only home here designed to be left.** Not "a premium apartment" — an ownership model. The competitor set sells buildings; this sells the eleven months you are not in it. |
| **Value proposition** | *Own it from anywhere. Leave it without worrying.* Remote purchase under registered Power of Attorney, remote handover, managed upkeep, and a rental desk if wanted. **Phase 3 found no competitor in the screened set offering any of this.** |
| **Likely demand** | **Real but must be created, not harvested.** Phase 5 §2.5: 60%+ of NRI buyers currently target Hyderabad, NCR or Bengaluru — Amaravati is not a top-tier NRI destination. The pool is large (1.23 million Telugu-origin people in the US alone; APNRTS headquartered in Guntur); the active slice is small. **This is the community whose demand assumption is weakest and whose acquisition cost is highest.** |
| **Revenue contribution** | **₹112.5 Cr — 22.2% of revenue from 22.3% of units.** ₹1.34 Cr per unit. |
| **Strategic role** | **Two jobs, both strategic rather than volumetric.** (1) **Capture the 45%.** Phase 5 §6.2 found that S3 and S7 together take 45% of top-end revenue and are absentee — this community is the product built for that fact rather than around it. (2) **Build an operating capability the competition cannot copy from a brochure.** A managed-ownership model costs an operating model, not a campaign. Once running, it is the hardest thing in this scheme to replicate — and it is reusable across every future KMV project. |

### 4.5 Community C — The Compact Villas

*The direct answer to the corridor's most disruptive competitor.*

| Attribute | |
| --- | --- |
| **Product type** | **Detached villas, G+1**, on ~200–225 sq yd plots. Private garden on all sides, two covered car spaces, Vaastu-compliant orientation as standard. |
| **Unit size** | 2,600 – 3,000 sq ft built-up |
| **Price band** | **₹6,500 / sq ft — a deliberate +20% over Prime Grandeur's ₹5,400.** |
| **Ticket size** | **₹1.69 – 1.95 Cr** — starting **above** Prime Grandeur's midpoint, ending near its ceiling. |
| **Land allocation** | **6.0 acres, 24% of site, 10 units/acre** |
| **Inventory logic** | 60 units. **Release slowest of the four.** Villa inventory in this corridor is scarce — Phase 2 §3.1 found no villa community above ~70 units and land parcels of 4.75–11.69 acres. Scarcity is the pricing mechanism; releasing in **four tranches of 15** preserves it. |
| **Primary audience** | **S1 — Vijayawada and Guntur trade and business families** (Phase 5, ₹121.5 Cr across the top of the scheme). Clears the ₹52 lakh cash gate more easily than any other resident segment. |
| **Secondary audience** | S2 senior AIIMS faculty and dual-doctor households · S6 upgraders at the top of their range · S7 Hyderabad buyers wanting ground |
| **Customer need** | **Ground.** Space, a private garden, parking for two to three vehicles, a formal receiving area, Vaastu compliance, and a home that reads as a house rather than a flat. Phase 5 §3 S1: space and standing outrank amenity lists and efficiency. |
| **Purchase trigger** | A good trading season. A daughter's or son's marriage. **A peer in the same trade buying first** — Phase 5 found this is the decisive trigger for S1. A land sale. Guideline-value revisions that make waiting more expensive. |
| **Positioning** | **A villa inside a masterplan, not a villa on a plot.** Prime Grandeur is 10 acres at 11.9 units/acre. This is 25 acres at 10 units/acre in this community and 15.0 across the scheme, with a central open space, clubhouse, and retail spine no 10-acre parcel can carry. |
| **Value proposition** | *The same form, in a place that will still be worth living in when it is finished.* The +20% rate premium buys 2.5× the land, half the neighbours per acre, and a delivery record Phase 4 established the competitor set cannot match — **KMV Projects built AIIMS Mangalagiri, three kilometres away.** |
| **Likely demand** | **The strongest evidenced demand in the scheme.** Prime Grandeur's 119 villas prove the form sells at this ticket in this exact micro-market. The question is not whether the product sells; it is whether it sells at +20%. |
| **Revenue contribution** | **₹109.2 Cr — 21.6% of revenue from 16.0% of units.** ₹1.82 Cr per unit. |
| **Strategic role** | **The competitive answer.** Every other community is positioned into open or lightly-held territory. C is the one that meets an incumbent head-on, and it does so on the two dimensions a 25-acre owner can win: masterplan scale and delivery credibility. **If C sells at +20%, the whole premium thesis is validated. If it does not, D's pricing is in question too** — which makes C the scheme's most informative early indicator. |

### 4.6 Community D — The Estate Villas

*The margin engine, placed in the emptiest price territory the research found.*

| Attribute | |
| --- | --- |
| **Product type** | **Detached estate villas, G+1 or G+2**, on ~300–350 sq yd plots. Private garden to all four sides, three to four car spaces, servant provision, and plot-level privacy the compact villas do not have. |
| **Unit size** | 3,200 – 3,800 sq ft built-up |
| **Price band** | **₹7,200 / sq ft** — inside Phase 2's empty ₹5,900–7,300 band, **below TAG AIRA's ₹7,309** and **13% below KMV Vivaan's ₹8,214–8,271**. |
| **Ticket size** | **₹2.30 – 2.74 Cr** — squarely inside the **₹2.2–2.8 crore band Phase 2 §3.1 found essentially empty**, and below TAG AIRA's ₹2.79 Cr entry. |
| **Land allocation** | **9.0 acres, 36% of site — the largest allocation — at 7 units/acre** |
| **Inventory logic** | 63 units. **Anchored to the corridor's own evidence:** KMV Vivaan achieved 66 villas on 10 acres at 6.6 units/acre; this is 63 on 9 acres at 7.0. Release in **four tranches of 15–16**, with the first tranche **seeded rather than advertised** (Phase 5 §5.4: S1 does not buy a project, it buys the other buyers). |
| **Primary audience** | **S1 — Vijayawada and Guntur trade and business families.** Phase 5: they clear ₹74.1 lakh in cash more easily than any other resident group, and frequently buy as **multiple related households in the same project**. |
| **Secondary audience** | S3 US diaspora wanting a villa rather than a managed residence · S7 Hyderabad households · **S4 land-pooling landowners entering through an exchange structure** rather than a cash purchase |
| **Customer need** | Permanence, standing, and space for an extended family — including the formal receiving room, the parking, and the servant provision that a compact villa cannot carry. For S4: a finished, liveable asset obtained without capital outlay. |
| **Purchase trigger** | A land sale or a strong trading year. A marriage in the family. **A respected peer buying first.** For S4: plot handover completion, or a neighbour completing an exchange successfully. |
| **Positioning** | **The largest homes in the corridor's least crowded price band.** Not "luxury" — Phase 5 §4 established that ₹2.74 crore is an upper-affluent ticket, not a high-net-worth one, and claiming otherwise promises a tier the price sheet does not deliver. |
| **Value proposition** | *Estate-scale living below the price the luxury tier starts at.* Seven homes per acre. A ticket that stops short of TAG AIRA's ₹2.79 crore entry while offering more land per home than anything at ₹1.8 crore. |
| **Likely demand** | **The scheme's biggest single unknown.** Phase 2 §3.1 was explicit: *whether the ₹2.2–2.8 crore band is empty because nobody has tried, or because buyers refuse it, cannot be determined from listing data alone.* Phase 5 narrowed it — the segments that clear ₹2.5 crore in cash (S1, S3, S7) are all real and all present in the catchment — but narrowing is not proving. |
| **Revenue contribution** | **₹158.8 Cr — 31.3% of revenue from 16.8% of units.** ₹2.52 Cr per unit, **3.4× Community A's**. |
| **Strategic role** | **The margin engine and the price anchor.** D sets the ceiling that makes C's +20% look reasonable and A's ₹5,150 look like access to something expensive. It also carries the **exchange structure for S4 landowners** — the one mechanism in this architecture that supplies land, absorbs inventory and creates local advocacy simultaneously (Phase 5 §5.4). **It is the highest-return and highest-risk community in the scheme, and those are the same fact.** |

## 5. The three questions the brief asks

### 5.1 "Why would a customer choose A rather than B?"

**For most buyers this is not a choice — and that is the point.** Community A tops out at **₹90 lakh**; Community B starts at **₹1.23 crore**. The gap is **₹33 lakh, or 36%**. The financial gate in Phase 5 §1.2 does the separating: an A buyer needs roughly **₹17.6–24 lakh in cash**, a B buyer needs **₹40 lakh**. Very few households sit on both sides of that line.

**The narrow band that does face the choice** is a household at ₹1.0–1.2 crore of capacity — a dual-income S6 upgrader, or an S2 assistant professor with a working spouse — deciding between the best unit in A and a stretch into B. For them:

| | Choose **A** if | Choose **B** if |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Occupancy** | You will live here daily | You will be away for long stretches |
| **Household** | Young children who need ground-level play space | No children at home, or ageing parents who need single-level living |
| **What you are buying** | **More home for the money, at half the corridor's density** | **Less home, plus an operating service that keeps it intact when you are not in it** |
| **The honest trade** | You handle your own maintenance | You pay for management you would not need if you were here |

> **Reasoned.** The separation is **occupancy, not aspiration.** A is not "the cheap one" and B is not "the better one" — B is worse value per square foot for a resident family and better value for an absent owner. **Selling B to a resident family is how this architecture would fail**, because that buyer discovers within a year that they paid for a service they do not use.

### 5.2 "Why would someone choose C rather than D?"

This is the sharper question, because both are detached villas on the same site with the same specification language. The answer is not the house.

**Computed** — what the ₹35 lakh step between C and D actually buys:

| | **C — Compact villa** | **D — Estate villa** | The step |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Built-up | 2,600 – 3,000 sq ft | 3,200 – 3,800 sq ft | **+600 to 800 sq ft** |
| Plot | ~200 – 225 sq yd | ~300 – 350 sq yd | **+100 to 125 sq yd** |
| Density | 10 / acre | 7 / acre | **30% fewer neighbours** |
| Parking | 2 covered | 3 – 4 covered | |
| Ticket | ₹1.69 – 1.95 Cr | ₹2.30 – 2.74 Cr | **+₹35 L at the join** |

> **Established, and it is the cleanest answer in this architecture: D does not sell more house. It sells more land.**
>
> The built-up difference is about 25%. The plot difference is about 50%. **Roughly two-thirds of the price step is land, not construction.**

**Why that works on this specific audience.** Phase 5 §3 S1 found that the primary audience for both C and D — Vijayawada and Guntur trade and business families — has as its rational driver *"land appreciation logic applied to built product"*, and as its default alternative *"why not just buy land?"*. **D answers that objection inside the project rather than losing to it outside.** A segment that already thinks in square yards is being sold square yards, in the one form that also comes with a house on it.

**And the guard against the reverse.** A C buyer is not a failed D buyer. C is the right product for a nuclear family that wants ground without maintaining 350 square yards of it — Phase 5's S2 senior faculty and dual-doctor households, whose scarcest resource is time.

### 5.3 "How can the four communities avoid cannibalising one another?"

Cannibalisation happens when two products compete for the same buyer's single purchase. Five mechanisms, in descending order of how much work each does.

**1 · Ticket gaps at every join, none of them narrow.**

| Join | Lower tops at | Upper starts at | **Gap** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A → B | ₹90 L | ₹1.23 Cr | **₹33 L (+36%)** |
| B → C | ₹1.45 Cr | ₹1.69 Cr | **₹24 L (+16%)** |
| C → D | ₹1.95 Cr | ₹2.30 Cr | **₹35 L (+18%)** |

**B → C is the flashpoint** — the narrowest gap, and the only join where the products are genuinely comparable in size. It needs two hard pricing rules, stated now rather than discovered later:

> **Rule 1.** No Community B unit is ever priced above **₹1.50 crore**, whatever the floor premium or view.
> **Rule 2.** No Community C villa is ever discounted below **₹1.65 crore**, whatever the quarter-end pressure.
>
> Breaking either rule closes the gap and turns a ₹24 lakh separation into a direct comparison the apartment loses.

**2 · A binary attribute at every join, not a gradient.** A buyer can argue about ₹24 lakh. They cannot argue about whether a home is detached.

| Join | The binary |
| --- | --- |
| A → B | Managed / not managed |
| B → C | **Detached / not detached** |
| C → D | **Large plot / compact plot** — and the plot boundary is fixed at the masterplan stage, so it cannot drift |

**3 · Different occupancy models.** B is the only community designed to be empty. That is not a feature comparison; it is a different use of a home.

**4 · Different triggers and different channels.** Phase 5 established these independently, and they barely overlap:

| | Trigger | Channel |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **A** | Pay-revision arrears; rent revision | Portals, search, departmental word-of-mouth |
| **B** | The December–January or summer India visit | Diaspora digital, APNRTS, community associations |
| **C** | A trading season; a peer buying | Referral, personal introduction, site visit |
| **D** | A land sale; a marriage; a peer buying | Referral, seeded; village relations for S4 |

**Two communities marketed on different calendars, in different languages, to different countries, are not competing for the same buyer.**

**5 · Sequenced release, so the scheme does not compete with itself.**

> **Reasoned — the real cannibalisation risk in this project is not between communities. It is between tranches.** Four communities × three to four tranches is up to fifteen inventory releases. Two open at once in adjacent bands means the sales team is arbitraging its own price list.
>
> **The rule: never hold open inventory in two adjacent communities at the same time.** A and C can be open together — ₹59 lakh and ₹1.7 crore do not compete. B and C must not be.

## 6. What makes this one development rather than four projects

Distinct propositions create a risk the brief implies but does not state: four communities that share nothing read as four small projects on one site, which forfeits the scale advantage that is the whole basis of the price premium (§2.3).

**Reasoned — three things bind it, and one thing must not.**

**Bound by a shared spine.** One gate and one address. A central open space that every community fronts onto rather than backs away from. One main clubhouse, one retail row, one school site, one sports zone. **The 25 acres has to be legible as 25 acres from inside any of the four communities** — that is what a 5-to-11-acre competitor cannot answer, and it only works if the open space is central and shared rather than divided four ways.

**Bound by shared infrastructure and one operator.** Security, STP, water, power backup, waste, landscaping — one system, one facilities operator, one standard. This also makes Community B's managed-ownership service economically possible: it is an *increment* on an operator that already exists, not a standalone business.

**Bound by an upgrade path.** An A buyer should have a documented internal move-up right — priority allocation and a transfer mechanism — into C or D in a later phase. Phase 5 found S6 upgraders are a real segment; **the cheapest upgrader to acquire is one who already lives inside the gate.**

**Not bound by a single amenity charge.** This is the failure mode to avoid. If all four communities pay into one maintenance pool, D's 63 households subsidise A's 169, and D buyers — a segment Phase 5 found highly attentive to who else is buying — will notice. **The structure should be a shared-spine charge that everyone pays on a per-square-foot basis, plus community-level charges for community-level amenities, plus an opt-in managed-service charge that exists only in B.** Three layers, transparent, priced separately.

## 7. Risks, and what would falsify this structure

| # | Risk | Exposure | Falsification test |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1** | **The ₹2.2–2.8 Cr band is empty because buyers refuse it**, not because nobody tried (Phase 2 §3.1, unresolved) | **₹158.8 Cr — 31% of revenue** in Community D | Price-ladder testing with S1 and S3 respondents. Booking velocity of any competitor entering the band. **The single highest-value test in this phase.** |
| **2** | **Community C's +20% over Prime Grandeur does not hold** | ₹109.2 Cr — 22% of revenue, and it also undermines D's anchor | C's first tranche of 15. If they do not clear at ₹6,500/sq ft within two quarters, the premium thesis is wrong and D must be repriced too |
| **3** | **The managed-residence proposition is unproven in this market** | ₹112.5 Cr — 22% of revenue, and the highest acquisition cost in the scheme (Phase 7 §6) | B is deliberately the smallest community (84 units, 3.5 acres) and releases in two tranches for exactly this reason. Second tranche gated on the first |
| **4** | **Density gets "optimised" later** | The entire price premium (§2.3) | Any proposal to raise FAR or add a tower. It should be treated as a proposal to cut price, because arithmetically it is |
| **5** | **APCRDA zoning at the chosen micro-location does not permit this mix** | Structural | The land model assumes gross FAR **0.76** against a permitted ceiling reported at up to 2.5 — a wide margin — but plot-specific zoning, road widths and setbacks are unverified. **Verify before anything else in this phase is acted on** |
| **6** | **Low-rise garden apartments cost more per sq ft to build** than the mid-rise this market builds | Community A margin, ₹126.2 Cr of revenue | A construction-cost estimate at G+3 versus G+9. Lower structural and fire-services cost may offset lower land efficiency; this phase has no cost data and does not claim it does |
| **7** | **Community A at 26 units/acre is still 3× the density of the rest of the scheme** | The "half the corridor's density" claim in A's positioning | It is true against corridor apartment schemes (52.9–113.4) and false against this scheme's own average of 15.0. **The claim must always be made against the competitor set, never against the project** |

### 7.1 The concentration to state plainly

**Computed.** ₹268.0 crore — **53% of revenue** — sits in Communities C and D, at rates of ₹6,500 and ₹7,200 per square foot. **Both are above every apartment rate ever recorded in this corridor, and both sit in or above a band Phase 2 could not prove is occupied.**

That is the structure's central bet, and it is deliberate: §1.3 and §3.1 established that the alternative — putting the area in the apartment band — means competing with roughly 3,269 units and, at the ₹1.3–1.9 crore ticket, against a detached villa priced at ₹5,400. **The concentration is not an oversight. It is the considered choice of the less crowded risk.** It should be held knowingly, and Risk 1 tested before the masterplan is frozen.

## 9. Carry-forward

**Established.**
- **Configuration is not a pricing lever here.** 2BHK and 3BHK bands overlap almost completely; size and address price, bedroom count does not.
- **Built form is the only in-project rate lever the evidence supports** — a ~30% step from the apartment ceiling to the villa band.
- **A premium apartment at ₹1.4–1.8 crore in this corridor competes against a detached villa at ₹5,400/sq ft and loses on form while charging 16% more per square foot.**
- **₹500 crore is not the capacity of 25 acres.** At the lowest apartment density observed nearby the land would yield ~₹851 crore; at mid density ~₹1,013 crore.

**Computed.**
- Recommended structure: **376 units, 8.31 lakh sq ft, gross FAR 0.76, 15.0 units/acre, blended ₹6,098/sq ft, ₹506.7 crore.**
- Land: **A 6.5 ac · B 3.5 ac · C 6.0 ac · D 9.0 ac.** Apartment forms 40%, villa forms 60%.
- Revenue: **A ₹126.2 Cr · B ₹112.5 Cr · C ₹109.2 Cr · D ₹158.8 Cr.**
- Ticket gaps at the joins: **+36% · +16% · +18%.**
- Sales task: **376 units — 7.8 to 10.4 bookings a month** over a 36–48 month window.

**Reasoned, and carried as working positions.**
- **The premium is purchased with density, not manufactured by marketing.** Protecting 15.0 units/acre is a pricing decision, not a planning one.
- **B and C must never be allowed to converge**: B capped at ₹1.50 Cr, C floored at ₹1.65 Cr.
- **Never hold open inventory in two adjacent communities simultaneously.**
- **C's first tranche is the scheme's most informative early indicator** — it tests the premium thesis against a live competitor before D's pricing is committed.

**Open.** The seven risks in §7 — of which **Risk 5 (zoning verification)** must be closed before anything here is acted on, and **Risk 1 (the empty band)** carries 31% of revenue and is the highest-value thing to test.
