§0

What this is, and what it is not

A reconstruction of the buyer universe from three things that can be established. It can size and rank segments. It is not a substitute for talking to buyers.

No surveys, no interviews, no focus groups, no CRM data, no walk-in analysis. That is the largest gap in this assignment and it is stated first rather than buried. What replaces it:

1 · The financial gate
  • Who can arithmetically afford each ticket band
  • Computed from published RBI loan-to-value rules and lender FOIR norms
  • Yields a hard filter before any behavioural claim is made
2 · Income sources that exist
  • Published salary scales — AIIMS, pay commissions, private universities
  • District income data and the Guntur trade economy
  • The land-pooling settlement and diaspora population counts
3 · National buyer research
  • Applied for direction, never for magnitude
  • Knight Frank Beyond Bricks 2025 — n=1,629, eight metros
  • None of those metros is Vijayawada, Guntur or Amaravati

Before asking who wants each product, establish who can pay for it. This filters the universe hard, and it is computable.

The consequence that shapes everything after this

The cash barrier discriminates more sharply than the income test — and it discriminates by income source, not income amount.

Two households with identical ₹30 lakh incomes — one salaried, one trading — have completely different abilities to put ₹52 lakh on a table, completely different documentation, and completely different objections. Established This is why the segmentation in §3 is built on where the money comes from rather than on life stage or geography.

Second consequence: Community D requires household income around ten times district per capita GDP — NTR district ₹3,60,354, Guntur ₹3,04,175. It cannot be sold primarily to salaried locals. It requires business income, diaspora income, professional income at the top of institutional scales, or asset conversion.

A correction, before it becomes a media plan

In this catchment the high-income institution is AIIMS — not the private universities.

SRM University AP holds a 100-acre campus at Neerukonda and VIT-AP sits beside the Secretariat, which makes "the university cluster" look like an affluent housing pool. Established The published pay does not support it: SRM Assistant Professor pay averages roughly ₹57,000 a month — about ₹6.8 lakh a year, below the Community A threshold.

Academic staff at SRM and VIT are a rental and entry-housing population, not a premium buyer pool. Any plan treating the university cluster as one affluent segment is wrong on the numbers.

Government income, and a dated trigger

Established The 8th Central Pay Commission was notified on 3 November 2025, effective 1 January 2026, with an expected 30–34% hike; rollout with retrospective arrears is expected to extend into 2027. Andhra Pradesh has constituted a Ninth Pay Revision Commission.

Reasoned Government employment reaches Community A comfortably and Community B at senior grades. It does not reach C or D on salary alone. But it is the most loan-friendly income in the catchment, and a pay revision with arrears is a purchase trigger — it creates exactly the lump sum that clears the down-payment barrier in §1.

Trade income — the deepest local pool

Established Guntur's chilli, tobacco and cotton ecosystem alone moves USD 4–5 billion. AP income-tax filers rose from 25.99 to 28.70 lakh. Purchasing power in this group is materially higher than declared income, which matters more for the cash gate than for the income test.

The land-pooling landowners — the source an outsider would miss

Established 33,008+ acres pooled from approximately 25,000 farmer households, average holding ~1.3 acres. Each surrendered acre returns roughly 1,000 sq yd of residential plot plus 250–450 sq yd commercial, against an annuity of ₹30–50,000 per acre per year for ten years, rising 10% annually.

Computed: at prevailing rates of ₹23,000–70,000 per sq yd, a one-acre household holds ₹2.3–7.0 crore of residential land plus ₹1 crore or more of commercial — against an annual cash income often under ₹2 lakh.

The structural anomaly

These households are land-rich and cash-thin. They clear Communities C and D on assets and fail Community A on income.

They cannot be sold to with a conventional EMI proposition. They can be sold to with land-for-home exchange, joint development, or part-consideration in kind — which is what Phase 4 found KMV's published /land-owners page exists to do, and which no competitor in the Phase 3 screened set offers.

Diaspora income — and the split that matters

Established The Telugu diaspora is estimated at ~3 million worldwide; an independent count gives ~2.3 million, of which 1,230,000 in the United States and 388,000 in Saudi Arabia. APNRTS, the state's non-resident agency, is headquartered in Guntur — inside the catchment.

Established — the reality check. A national survey found 60%+ of NRI respondents target Hyderabad, NCR or Bengaluru. Amaravati is not a top-tier NRI destination. Any plan assuming NRI demand arrives on its own is wrong; here it has to be created, against three cities with better-known markets.

Reasoned US-based and Gulf-based Telugu households are two different segments with two different products. The US pool is technology-weighted and clears Communities C and D. The Gulf pool is remittance-weighted and clears A and B. Phase 3 found every competitor collapsing them into one "NRI" audience — which spends the Gulf budget on a product that audience cannot buy, and under-serves the audience that can.
§3

The eight segments that actually exist

The brief's fifteen labels are three segmentation axes overlaid on each other. Profiling all fifteen would profile the same households three times.

AxisHypotheses using it
Life stageFirst-time buyers · young professionals · young couples · growing families · upgraders
AffluenceEstablished business families · affluent professionals · HNIs · investors
GeographyLocal buyers · Vijayawada · Guntur · Amaravati-region · outside AP · NRIs

A growing family in Vijayawada is simultaneously a life-stage hypothesis, a geography hypothesis and — depending on what the household does for a living — an affluence hypothesis. Segments built that way are not mutually exclusive and cannot be used to allocate a rupee of media spend.

The organising decision

Established The discriminator in this catchment is income source, not income amount.

§1 showed why: the binding constraint is cash at purchase. Two households with identical ₹30 lakh incomes — one salaried, one trading — have different abilities to clear that gate, different documentation, different objections and different decision timelines.

So the primary axis is where the money comes from, and life stage and geography become descriptors within each segment rather than segments in their own right. That produces eight mutually exclusive, individually addressable segments, each traceable to an income source in §2.

How to read the profiles. Each covers the twenty attributes the brief asks for. Attributes 1–7 — demographics through financial capacity — are Established. S1

Vijayawada & Guntur trade and business families

The deepest pool of purchase-ready cash in the catchment.

HIGH Score 4.05 Horizon Near · 3–9 months
Size — Reasoned, order of magnitude only. AP has 28.70 lakh income-tax filers. NTR and Guntur districts hold roughly 8% of state population but a disproportionate share of filers, being urban and trade-centred. The slice clearing both the ₹25 lakh income test and the ₹52 lakh cash test is a small fraction of that. A bounding exercise, not a count — §7 flags it.
Demographics
Principal buyer 38–58. Business owner, commission agent, wholesaler, transport operator, jeweller, hospitality, construction supplier. Education varies widely and correlates poorly with wealth.
Geography
Vijayawada — Governorpet, Labbipet, Benz Circle, Patamata. Guntur — Brodipet, Arundelpet, Lakshmipuram. Also Tenali, Mangalagiri town, Chilakaluripet.
Income & power
Household income frequently ₹25 lakh–1 crore+, but partly undocumented. Guntur's chilli, tobacco and cotton ecosystem alone moves USD 4–5 billion. Purchasing power exceeds declared income.
Family structure
Joint or semi-joint. Two to four adults contributing. Often multiple related households buying in the same project.
Life stage
Consolidating. Children in senior school, college or newly married. Frequently buying for the next generation.
Housing requirement
3BHK large, 4BHK or villa. Space, parking for 2–4 vehicles and a formal receiving area matter more than efficiency. Vaastu compliance close to non-negotiable. Servant provision assumed.
Financial capacity
Clears Communities C and D comfortably on cash — the ₹52–74 lakh test is the easiest in this document for them. Loan used selectively, for tax or working-capital reasons, not from necessity.
Purchase motivation
Converting business cash into a hard, visible asset. Status confirmation within a tight, observant peer network. Providing for children. Rental yield is close to irrelevant.
Pain points
Documentation and title anxiety — this segment has seen local disputes. Distrust of developers who sell and disappear. Reluctance to expose financial detail to a bank.
Objections
“Who else has bought?” is the first objection and the most important one in this document. Then: will it get built, what is the resale, and why not just buy land — land being their default asset class.
Purchase triggers
A good trading season. A daughter's or son's marriage. A peer in the same trade buying first. A land sale. Guideline-value revisions that make waiting more expensive.
Decision factors
Developer track record above everything. Then location legibility — “everyone knows where that is” — then unit size, then who the neighbours will be. Amenity lists rank low.
Emotional drivers
Standing. Permanence. Being seen to have arrived, and to have arrived in the capital region. Legacy for children.
Rational drivers
Land-appreciation logic applied to built product. Capital preservation. Proximity to existing business premises — this segment will not move far from its trade base.
Preferred channels
Referral and personal introduction first. Then a physical site visit. Then print and hoardings for legitimacy. Digital is a verification channel, not a discovery channel.
Information sources
Trade peers, family, the family advocate and chartered accountant, local brokers, the sub-registrar network. Reasoned The CA and the advocate are gatekeepers, and no Phase 3 competitor addresses either.
Digital behaviour
WhatsApp-primary, YouTube for long-form. Will search the developer's name and read page one — which is where Phase 4's finding on thin search visibility bites hardest. Unlikely to fill a portal form; likely to call a hoarding number.
Site-visit behaviour
Visits with family, usually 3–6 people, often unannounced, frequently on a Sunday. Expects the decision-maker on site. Two or three visits before committing, elders present on the confirming one.
Decision timeline
3–9 months. Long consideration, then a sudden close once the family aligns. Deposit to agreement can be days.
Influencers
Spouse, father or eldest brother, the family CA, and one respected peer who has already bought. Applied The decision is collective; here the collective is wider than the nuclear family.
The decisive insight

This segment does not buy a project; it buys the other buyers. The first fifteen sales into a recognised local network determine whether the next hundred are easy or impossible.

That has a direct launch consequence: seed the premium tiers before advertising them.

S2

Senior institutional medical professionals

The most reliably documented high income in the corridor, and the one most tied to the micro-location.

HIGH Score 3.90 Horizon Near · 6 weeks–4 months
Size — Reasoned. AIIMS Mangalagiri at full faculty strength, plus NRI Academy of Sciences at Chinakakani, Katuri and Guntur Medical Colleges, and the Vijayawada private hospital cluster. Households clearing Community B or above are order of low thousands; the slice clearing D is a few hundred. §7 flags it.
Demographics
32–55. Consultants, faculty, super-specialists, senior residents at the entry end. Often two doctors in one household — which changes the arithmetic completely.
Geography
Already living in Mangalagiri, Chinakakani, Tadepalli and Vijayawada. This segment already commutes across the exact corridor the project sits in.
Income & power
Senior Resident ₹11–13 L → A. Assistant Professor ₹24–26 L → B. Additional Professor ₹36–52 L → C/D. Super-specialty Professor ₹36–48 L → D. A dual-doctor household at Assistant Professor level clears C.
Family structure
Nuclear, one or two children, frequently a dependent parent. Both adults working, often on incompatible shift patterns.
Life stage
Establishing or consolidating. Many are recent arrivals — AIIMS Mangalagiri is a young institution — which makes them actively in-market rather than latently in-market.
Housing requirement
3BHK or compact 4BHK. Commute time to the hospital is the hardest constraint — a clinician on call cannot live 40 minutes away. Security, a study, quiet and reliable power rank above clubhouse scale.
Financial capacity
Highly loan-eligible: documented, stable, salaried, lender-favoured. But cash-constrained — clearing a ₹94,800 EMI and finding ₹52 lakh in hand are different problems. Payment structuring matters more than price here.
Purchase motivation
Ending the commute. Converting rent into ownership. A permanent base after a career of institutional transfers. Schooling for children.
Pain points
Time, genuinely — not a figure of speech for a clinician. Cannot do multi-week diligence. Cannot attend weekday site visits. Frustrated by processes requiring repetition.
Objections
“Will it be delivered on time?” — delay costs them rent and disruption at once. “Is the commute really 12 minutes?” “What are the schools like?” “Am I overpaying versus Vijayawada?”
Purchase triggers
Confirmation in post, or promotion from Senior Resident to faculty. A child reaching school age. Rent revision. Spouse's job confirmation. A colleague buying — this is a small, highly connected community.
Decision factors
Commute time, delivery credibility, build quality, school access, security. Applied National buyer work finds professional buyers weighting delivery record above amenity depth; the direction transfers even if the magnitude does not.
Emotional drivers
Stability after years of transfers. Belonging in a place they moved to for work. A peer-appropriate address. Relief from logistics.
Rational drivers
Rent-to-EMI arithmetic. Loan eligibility they know they possess. Tax deduction. Commute distance measured, not described.
Preferred channels
Digital-first, and here that is actually true. Search, portals, developer website, WhatsApp for follow-up. Responsive to precise information, averse to sales pressure.
Information sources
Google, 99acres / MagicBricks / Housing, RERA lookup, colleague referral, resident WhatsApp groups. Reasoned This segment will check RERA. Most of the catchment will not.
Digital behaviour
Researches at night and between shifts. Reads specification detail. Compares 3–5 projects on a spreadsheet. Will watch a six-minute walkthrough. Responds to email; often cannot take calls during the day.
Site-visit behaviour
Weekday evening or Sunday, by appointment, couple only. One focused visit and one confirming visit. Wants the drive from the hospital timed on the way. Family comes on the second visit.
Decision timeline
6 weeks to 4 months — the shortest of the affluent segments, because the trigger is usually dated: a posting, a school year, a lease expiry.
Influencers
Spouse — co-equal, often primary on the home itself. Then colleagues who have bought, then parents on the financial question.
The structural link to Phase 4

Phase 4 established that KMV Projects built AIIMS Mangalagiri. For the one segment that most values delivery credibility, and whose workplace is that building, this is not a claim to be written — it is a verifiable fact standing three kilometres from the site.

No competitor in the Phase 3 screened set has anything comparable to say to this audience.

S3

US and developed-market Telugu diaspora households

The largest latent pool, the smallest active one. The gap between the two is the opportunity.

HIGH Score 4.20 Horizon Mid · 6–18 months
Size — Established at pool level, Reasoned at addressable level. 1,230,000 Telugu-origin people in the United States — roughly 400,000 households — plus Canada, Australia and the UK. But 60%+ of NRI buyers target Hyderabad, NCR or Bengaluru. The active Amaravati-considering slice is small and must be created rather than harvested.
Demographics
33–52. Technology, healthcare, finance, academia. H-1B, green card and naturalised citizens. Household income frequently USD 150,000–400,000.
Geography
New Jersey, the Bay Area, Dallas, Chicago, Atlanta, Seattle. Family origin in Krishna, Guntur, West Godavari, Prakasam — origin district matters far more for targeting than current city.
Income & power
Clears Community D on income without strain. The constraint is allocation, not affordability: this purchase competes with a US mortgage, college funds and Hyderabad property.
Family structure
Nuclear abroad, with parents still resident in AP — usually Vijayawada, Guntur or the home village. That parental household is central to everything below.
Life stage
Settled abroad, ten to twenty years out. Parents ageing. Beginning to think about a permanent Indian anchor.
Housing requirement
Villa or large premium apartment. Lock-and-leave security is the defining requirement — the home will be empty eleven months a year. Maintenance handled by someone else. Space for parents, capacity for the annual visit.
Financial capacity
Very strong. NRE/NRO repatriation, PoA execution and FEMA compliance are the friction, not the money. Purchase can be completed entirely remotely under a registered Power of Attorney.
Purchase motivation
Four, and they differ: a home for ageing parents; a return or retirement anchor; currency-hedged asset allocation; identity and a stake in the state capital. Note that the first makes the parents the actual occupant.
Pain points
Cannot verify anything personally. Cannot inspect. Depends on a relative for site visits — whose opinion becomes decisive. Time-zone friction in every conversation. Has heard the horror stories.
Objections
“How do I know this is real?” — fraud is the stated primary NRI concern. Then: who maintains it when empty, can I sell it if I never return, and “why Amaravati and not Hyderabad?” — the real competitor here is a city, not a project.
Purchase triggers
An India visit — December–January and summer, and those windows are non-negotiable. A parent's health event. A green card or citizenship milestone. A bonus or vesting event. A sibling buying.
Decision factors
Developer credibility and legal cleanliness above all. Then remote-management capability, then rental and resale exit, then the capital-city story. Amenities matter less here than to any other segment — the household will not use them.
Emotional drivers
Guilt and duty toward parents. Nostalgia for the region. Wanting to be part of the capital's making rather than to watch it from abroad. Standing within the extended family.
Rational drivers
INR/USD diversification. Capital appreciation on a capital-city thesis. A physical base for eventual return. Rental income while empty.
Preferred channels
Digital, entirely. Search, YouTube, LinkedIn, Facebook and WhatsApp community groups, diaspora associations, video call. APNRTS is headquartered in Guntur — an institutional channel no Phase 3 competitor was found using.
Information sources
Google in English, YouTube walkthroughs, diaspora WhatsApp and Facebook groups, family in India, NRI property content, RERA verification.
Digital behaviour
Very high intent, very long research cycles, very low form-fill rates. Watches long video, reads at odd hours relative to India, will take a video walkthrough before a physical one, expects to transact digitally.
Site-visit behaviour
A proxy visit by a relative first, then a personal visit compressed into one India trip. The trip visit is decisive and there is no second chance — if the site is not ready to close in that window, the sale is lost for a year.
Decision timeline
6–18 months of consideration, then a five-day decision window during the India visit. This bimodal pattern is the most important operational fact about the segment.
Influencers
Spouse, then parents in India, then the sibling or cousin who does the physical inspection — who holds effective veto and is almost never the person the marketing is aimed at.
Two structural mistakes to avoid

1 · Treating “NRI” as one segment. The US and Gulf pools differ in size, income, purpose and product fit. Phase 3 found every competitor collapsing them.

2 · Campaigning to the diaspora on Indian seasonality. This segment's buying window is set by their travel calendar — December–January and summer. A launch calendar built on Dasara–Diwali will miss them entirely.

S4

Amaravati land-pooling landowner households

Structurally unlike every other segment here, and the one no competitor is built to serve.

SECONDARY Score 4.05 Horizon Long · 6–24 months
Size — Established. 33,008+ acres pooled from approximately 25,000 farmer households, average holding ~1.3 acres. Reasoned The tier holding one acre or more — order of 12,000–15,000 households — holds ₹2.3–7.0 crore in residential returnable plots plus commercial land, against an annuity of roughly ₹50,000–85,000 a year.
Demographics
40–70 principally, with sons and daughters aged 25–45 increasingly driving decisions. Agricultural background; many now without an operating farm, the land having been surrendered.
Geography
The 29 villages of the capital area — Thullur, Velagapudi, Rayapudi, Mandadam, Nelapadu, Lingayapalem, Venkatapalem, Nidamarru, Kuragallu, Neerukonda. They live on top of the recommended micro-location.
Income & power
The defining asymmetry: land-rich, cash-thin. Assets of ₹3–10 crore; annual cash income often under ₹2 lakh from the annuity. Clears C and D on net worth; fails A on income.
Family structure
Joint, multi-generational — often 6–12 people across three generations in one compound. Decisions are made by the family, not the individual.
Life stage
In forced transition. The occupation is gone, the land is committed, the returnable plots are being handed over. An entire community re-deciding how it lives — a once-in-a-generation moment that will not recur.
Housing requirement
Two distinct needs. The parent generation wants ground-connected living, space and continuity with village life — a villa, not a flat. The 25–45 generation, urban-employed or aspiring, wants a serviced apartment with security. One household may need both.
Financial capacity
The conventional structure does not work. No income statement, no ITR history, poor loan eligibility, and no need for a loan. What works: land-for-home exchange, joint development on the returnable plot, part-consideration in kind, or a plot-plus-cash swap.
Purchase motivation
Converting an illiquid, undevelopable plot into a finished, liveable or income-generating asset — without becoming a developer. Separate homes for sons. Re-establishing status where the old marker of it has been surrendered.
Pain points
Deep, evidence-based institutional distrust — the pooling process itself, the capital's stop-start history since 2019, and years of uncertainty before the April 2026 gazette notification. Fear of being outmanoeuvred a second time.
Objections
“What exactly do I get, and when?” “What happens if you don't build?” “Why should I hand over land again?” — emotionally loaded in a way no brochure answers, and the correct objection to have.
Purchase triggers
Plot handover completion. A neighbour completing an exchange successfully. A son's marriage. A specific, written, legally structured offer — this segment does not respond to advertising; it responds to a document.
Decision factors
Terms of exchange, legal enforceability, the developer's local standing, and precedent — has anyone in the village done this and been treated well?
Emotional drivers
Dignity. Vindication after a decade of uncertainty. Being a participant in the capital rather than a casualty of it. Provision for sons. Handled carelessly this is not a lost sale but a local reputational liability.
Rational drivers
Asset conversion without capital outlay. Avoiding the cost, risk and expertise of self-development. Income from a portion while retaining a portion.
Preferred channels
In person, in the village, in Telugu. Village elders, farmer associations, the local advocate, the surveyor. Not digital. Not advertising. A land-owner relations function, not a campaign.
Information sources
Neighbours, family, the village network, the CRDA office, local Telugu press and Telugu YouTube channels.
Digital behaviour
Telugu-language YouTube and WhatsApp forwarding are genuinely high here; formal web research is not. The younger generation researches on behalf of the elders.
Site-visit behaviour
Does not need to visit — they live there. What they need instead is to see completed work: a finished building, a delivered promise, someone they know who was paid what they were told they would be paid.
Decision timeline
6–24 months. Slow, consultative, and irreversible once made. Trust accrues over repeated contact, not over a campaign flight.
Influencers
The eldest male, the sons, the village elder, the family advocate — and the first neighbour to complete a transaction, who becomes the single most persuasive asset available.
A defensible white space, and Phase 4 already found the door

Phase 3 §5 identified land-owner engagement as a surviving white space — no competitor in the screened set offers a structured land-for-home proposition. Phase 4 §6.1 found KMV Spaces already publishes a /land-owners page. The asset exists and is unexploited.

Reasoned This segment does not primarily buy units off a price list — it enters through an exchange structure that supplies land, absorbs inventory and creates local advocacy at the same time. It is a supply-side and demand-side segment simultaneously, which is why it is worth more than its unit count suggests.

S5

Government, public-sector and the relocating capital workforce

The most loan-eligible income in the catchment, and the one with a dated trigger attached.

SECONDARY Score 2.95 Horizon Near · 3–8 months
Size — Insufficient public evidence. No published headcount was found for state employees relocating to Amaravati after the 6 April 2026 gazette notification. Estimates circulate; none traced to a primary source. §7 flags this as a priority gap, because the number materially changes Community A and B sizing.
Demographics
30–52. State government officers, central government staff, PSU and bank employees, teachers, police, judiciary support. Grade determines everything about the profile.
Geography
Currently Vijayawada, Guntur, Tadepalli and Mangalagiri; a portion still in Hyderabad and relocating.
Income & power
Reaches Community A comfortably and Community B at senior grades; does not reach C or D on salary alone. Dual-government households and those with agricultural land alongside reach further.
Family structure
Nuclear with one or two children, frequently supporting parents. Single earner in many cases; dual-government households are common and change the arithmetic.
Life stage
Establishing to consolidating. Many have rented through a decade of capital uncertainty and deferred buying because of it.
Housing requirement
2BHK and 3BHK. Gated security, reliable water and power, school access, covered parking. Efficient carpet area matters more than showpiece amenity — the budget is real and precisely known.
Financial capacity
The most loan-friendly income in this document — documented, stable, pensionable, often with subsidised institutional loan access. The constraint is the down payment, not the EMI.
Purchase motivation
Ending a long rental period. Buying before the capital's confirmation is fully priced in. Proximity to a workplace that is itself moving. Children's schooling.
Pain points
The down payment. Transfer risk — a posting can move them. A decade of watching the capital start, stop and restart, which has trained caution. Real sensitivity to ₹200–300/sq ft differences.
Objections
“Can I afford the down payment this year?” “What if I'm transferred?” “Is this priced above Vijayawada for the same thing?” “Will possession actually be in 2029?”
Purchase triggers
The pay revision. The 8th CPC is effective 1 January 2026 at an expected 30–34%, with arrears extending into 2027; AP's 9th PRC is constituted. Retrospective arrears are precisely the lump sum the cash gate demands.
Decision factors
Price per square foot, EMI affordability, possession date, commute, RERA registration, school access. This segment compares on a spreadsheet and will find a ₹150/sq ft difference.
Emotional drivers
Security. Ending the rent cycle. Respectability of address. Pride in the capital as a personal rather than abstract project.
Rational drivers
Rent versus EMI. Loan eligibility. Section 24 and 80C deductions. Buying ahead of an anticipated price rise.
Preferred channels
Mixed. Portals and search for shortlisting; WhatsApp and departmental word-of-mouth for validation. Print retains real credibility. Employer-adjacent and association channels are unusually effective and unusually cheap.
Information sources
Colleagues, portals, the developer site, RERA, local Telugu press, family.
Digital behaviour

The product correction

The Gulf pool is large, but its ticket capacity sits at Communities A and B while the US pool sits at C and D.

Three findings that survive as corrections to the intuitive reading

1 · “NRI” is two segments, not one — and the larger one by headcount buys the cheaper product while the smaller one buys the expensive one. Getting this backwards wastes the entire diaspora budget.

2 · “HNI” is not a segment in this catchment, and Community D at ₹2.57 crore is not an HNI product.

3 · The largest and most obvious segment is the most crowded. Phase 3's 8:1 apartment-to-villa ratio is aimed almost entirely at growing families upgrading in Vijayawada. Being obvious and being winnable are not the same thing.

Three things the reconciliation shows

1 · The three high-priority segments deliver 53.8% of revenue from 43% of units. The priority order and the revenue architecture agree. That is the check passing.

2 · The most contested segment delivers the least revenue per unit. S6 is 21% of units and 13.7% of revenue, at ₹0.83 crore per unit against S1's ₹1.74 crore and S3's ₹1.96 crore. Four things the derivation produced that assumption would not have

1 · Community D's largest buyer group will not live in it

S3 and S7 together take 45% of Community D — 35 units and ₹80.1 crore. Both are absentee owners.

Add the S4 parent generation, who will live there permanently, and Community D splits into two occupancy models inside one product.

Reasoned The consequence is operational, not creative. A villa community where nearly half the owners are absent needs managed maintenance, remote handover, security designed for empty homes, and optional rental management as part of the product. Phase 3 found no competitor offering this. It is a differentiator that costs an operating model rather than a marketing budget — which is why it is defensible.

2 · The largest unit count sits in the most contested band

Community B is 139 units — 35% of the scheme by count — at ₹5,400/sq ft, against a competitive set of roughly 3,269 mid-market apartment units.

This is the project's largest volume risk, and it was not visible until the audience work was done. Community D is the revenue engine with the thinnest competition; Community B is the volume engine with the thickest. The marketing effort those two require is not proportional to their revenue.

3 · Segment 1 is not “the villa audience”

S1's 70 units distribute as B 14 · C 29 · D 27. Trade and business families buy across the entire top half of the scheme, frequently as multiple related households in the same project.

That changes the sales approach from unit-selling to family-selling — and it is why seeding the premium tiers before advertising them matters more than any single campaign.

4 · One pay ladder reaches the whole product ladder

Segment 2 is the only segment present in all four communities — because AIIMS Mangalagiri's four published pay rungs map cleanly onto the four price tiers.

Senior Resident → A (~10 units) · Assistant Professor → B (~28) · Additional Professor → C (~21) · super-specialty Professor → D (~6). A single institutional relationship can feed the entire ladder, following a career-stage progression inside one workplace three kilometres from the site — built by the developer's own group company.

Established. No other segment has this property, and no competitor found in Phase 3 is positioned to claim it.

Gaps 1, 6 and 7 are the same gap seen three ways: nobody has spoken to a buyer.

  • The binding constraint is cash at purchase — ₹17.6 L for A rising to ₹74.1 L for D. It discriminates by income source, not amount.
  • Community D requires household income around 10× district per capita GDP. It cannot be sold primarily to salaried locals.
  • AIIMS is the corridor's high-income institution; the private universities are not.
  • The AIIMS pay ladder maps rung-for-rung onto Communities A–D.
  • “NRI” is two segments — US buys C and D, Gulf buys A and B.
  • “HNI” is not a segment here, and ₹2.57 crore is not an HNI ticket.
  • ~25,000 land-pooling households hold ₹2.3–7.0 crore of land each against annuity income under ₹2 lakh.
Correction to Phase 1
  • The sales task is ~397 units, not ~600. Average unit size rises from ~1,389 to ~2,098 sq ft on the Phase 2 mix.
  • Bookings needed fall to 8.3–11.0 a month.
  • Allowable marketing cost per unit rises from ₹3.3–4.2 lakh to ₹5.0–6.3 lakh.
Reasoned — working assumptions
  • High: S1 trade families · S2 senior medical · S3 US diaspora. Secondary: S4 landowners · S7 Hyderabad · S6 upgraders · S5 government. Low: S8 Gulf · investors.
  • The three high-priority segments deliver 53.8% of revenue from 43% of units.
  • 45% of Community D revenue comes from buyers who will not live there full time.
  • Community B is the largest volume risk — 139 units in the most contested band.
  • Two dated opportunities, both known now: the pay-revision arrears window and the December–January and summer India-visit windows.
