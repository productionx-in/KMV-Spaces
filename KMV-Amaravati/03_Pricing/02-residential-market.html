§0

Method

Why this is built bottom-up from projects rather than top-down from averages — and what that buys.

The brief asked for ranges, not a generic average. The data makes that mandatory rather than merely preferable: published locality averages for the same micro-market, in the same period, disagree by up to 40% (§1).

So this phase inverts the usual method. It starts from named, RERA-registered projects with published unit sizes and ticket prices, derives ₹/sq ft arithmetically, and builds segment bands from the resulting cloud of points. Three advantages:

  1. It is checkable. Every rate can be recomputed from a stated size and a stated price. The full derivation is in the appendix.
  2. It exposes contradictions. Where a portal's stated rate disagrees with its own price ÷ size, that is recorded rather than smoothed.
  3. It produces bands, not points — which is what a pricing decision actually needs.

These are not errors

They are different questions being answered. A locality average depends entirely on the basket: all stock vs new projects only; 2BHK vs all configurations; asking vs transacted; Mangalagiri classified as a Guntur-district town or a Vijayawada suburb. The three internal contradictions found

These matter because they show how portal data behaves, and they are why the derivation convention exists.

  • Manjeera Monarch. One portal states ₹4,800/sq ft; the same project's stated 3BHK range derives to ₹5,732–5,822; a third source gives ₹5,100 asking. The ₹4,800 is most likely a stale base rate excluding floor rise, parking, corpus and amenity charges. ₹5,100 is carried forward — the only source describing itself as a current asking rate.
  • Prime Hill Crest. Ticket ÷ size gives ₹4,491–4,500 at both ends; the same page separately states "average ₹5k/sq ft." The derived figure is preferred, because it is reproducible.
  • Raintree Park. Listed 2BHK "from ₹63 L" sits above the listed 3BHK band (₹50.58–56.29 L) — arithmetically impossible within one project. Treated as a portal data error; only the ~₹3,900 average is carried, at low confidence.
The general lesson, and it scales the whole revenue model

A stated ₹/sq ft on a portal is frequently a base rate. The ticket price is what a buyer pays.

All-in realisation typically runs 8–15% above base rate once floor rise, car parking, club membership, corpus fund and GST are added. Any pricing model built on portal base rates will understate achievable revenue.

Whether it is empty because nobody has tried, or because buyers refuse it, cannot be determined from listing data alone. Resolving that is the highest-value item in §15.

Villa project scale is uniformly small. The largest villa counts found are 66 (KMV Vivaan), 55 (TAG AIRA), 50 (Golden Villas), 26 (IJM), on parcels of 4.75–11.69 acres. No villa community above ~70 units was found anywhere in the corridor.

The gap between the two villa bands — ₹5,900 to ₹7,300, roughly the ₹2.2–2.8 crore ticket — is the emptiest territory in the corridor. Hover any band for its ticket range and anchoring projects.

Four things the ladder makes visible

1 · The 2BHK and mid-3BHK bands overlap almost completely (₹3,800–4,800 against ₹3,800–4,600). Moving from 2BHK to 3BHK here buys more area at roughly the same rate, not a better rate. Configuration is not a pricing lever in this corridor — size and address are.

2 · The step from mid to premium gated is ₹700–800/sq ft (≈18%). The step from premium apartments to luxury villas is roughly ₹2,000/sq ft (≈35%). The villa premium is where the pricing power sits, and it dwarfs any apartment-tier premium available.

3 · The apartment ceiling is ₹6,300–6,500/sq ft, set by Vertex Siris Signa — a 3-acre, 184-unit ready scheme in Vijayawada city. Nothing found in Mangalagiri or Tadepalli reaches it. The premium apartment ceiling is currently a city-centre phenomenon, not a corridor one. Whether it can be imported into the corridor is the central pricing risk for the hypothetical project's premium apartment communities.

4 · Villas break that ceiling by a wide margin. KMV Vivaan achieved ₹8,200/sq ft — 30% above the best apartment rate found anywhere in the region. Villas are the only product type in this evidence base that reliably clears ₹7,000.

The finding that reframes the product story

A 25-acre, ~475-unit scheme would not be the corridor's biggest by unit count — Aparna already delivers 612 and Raintree 632. It would be the biggest by land, by roughly 2.5×, and the least dense mixed project in the corridor by a factor of three to five.

That implied density is independently consistent with the 50:50 to 60:40 apartment/villa land split that Phase 1 derived from the ₹500 crore GDV. Two separate calculations converge on the same product shape — a meaningful consistency check.

A buyer comparing a ₹1.5 crore 3BHK here against Manjeera Monarch is choosing between roughly 20 homes per acre and 113.

That is a difference visible from the site gate — open space, distance between blocks, parking, tree cover, noise. It needs no brochure to explain, and it is the most defensible basis found for the price premium Phase 1 identified as this project's central problem.

How to read the +27%, honestly

The guideline-value revision took effect on 1 February 2026 — inside the same financial year. Registration revenue is charged on the higher of consideration or guideline value.

So a meaningful part of the +27% is the same number of transactions being taxed on higher recorded values, not more transactions. Volume growth and value growth cannot be separated from the published figure. And the department landed at 84.45% of its own target — the state itself expected more.

The defensible reading: transaction activity in AP is genuinely growing, at a rate somewhere below 27%, and the state's own forecast was not met.

Two cautions, both material

"Demand" here means search activity on a portal, not transactions. A 49.10% rise in people searching Amaravati listings after the capital was revived is entirely believable and tells you about attention. It does not tell you anyone bought anything. Corridor marketing routinely quotes the 49% figure as "residential demand growth" without that qualifier. It should not be used that way.

This data is from Q3 2024 — nearly two years stale. It predates the May 2025 relaunch, the April 2026 gazette notification and the February 2026 guideline revision. There is no equivalent 2026 read for this corridor, because these cities are not in the ongoing 13-city PropIndex panel. That is the single largest evidence gap in Phase 2.

What the Q3 2024 data does establish is a structural scissors — demand rising 32–49% while listings fell 7–23% — which is the mechanism behind Vijayawada's +20% price move. Whether that scissors is still open in 2026 is unknown.

National context, for calibration

Magicbricks PropIndex Q1 2026, 13 major cities: active supply +6.8% QoQ, +10.1% YoY to 110,821 listings; national average ₹14,633/sq ft, +14.1% YoY; demand −2.2% QoQ, +1.5% YoY. Largest supply increases: Bengaluru +22.3%, Hyderabad +19.8%.

Cross-read with Phase 1's Knight Frank data (unsold inventory +4% YoY to 525,695 units; ₹2–5 crore inventory +43%), the national picture is consistent: supply expanding faster than demand, with the build-up concentrated in premium. Note also that agencies disagree sharply — for Q1 2026 Knight Frank reported sales +2% while PropEquity and ANAROCK reported −23% and −28%. Any single agency number is one reading, not the reading.

Tadepalli, the capital-facing address, appreciated +34.2%. The capital story is not the corridor's best-performing real-estate trade. That does not make the capital irrelevant — but the appreciation argument cannot be attributed to Amaravati adjacency without evidence that does not currently exist.

Honest conclusion

Absorption in this corridor is unmeasured, and the two strongest available signals point in opposite directions.

Supply contraction and rising search demand suggest a tight market. A four-year-old ready project still selling, negotiable pricing in the premium band, and national premium inventory build-up suggest a slow one. Phase 3 pricing should be modelled against the slow case and treat the tight case as upside.

New launches and pipeline

Portal listing counts — not a supply register, and heavily double-counted across platforms: Mangalagiri 13–24 projects depending on source; Tadepalli 12+; Vijayawada 57+ under construction and 84+ upcoming; Guntur 66–174.

These establish that the pipeline is numerous and fragmented — dozens of small schemes rather than a handful of large ones — consistent with the density table in §5 and with Phase 1's finding of white space at 25-acre scale.

One broker source reports "over 2,500 units expected to enter the market in 2025–26" across the APCRDA region. Tier 6, uncorroborated — flagged, not relied on. If accurate, a 400–550 unit project would be roughly 16–22% of two years' regional supply, a material absorption consideration. Worth verifying properly before any launch decision.

The yield inversion

Tadepalli — the corridor's highest-priced micro-market — has its lowest rental yield. Kanuru, at materially lower capital values, yields 3.2% against Tadepalli's 2.2%.

That inversion is the classic signature of capital values running ahead of rental fundamentals. Tadepalli prices rose 34.2% over three years; rents did not follow.

Sense-check the arithmetic. A ₹1.5 crore 3BHK at 2.2% gross generates roughly ₹27,500/month before maintenance, vacancy and management — consistent with the observed ₹12,000–35,000 band. Net of costs that is a ~1.5–2.0% net yield. Against home loan rates of 7.15–7.35%, a leveraged buy-to-let in this corridor is materially cash-flow negative.

Implication 1
  • Investor messaging built on rental yield will not survive a spreadsheet. The investor case here is appreciation — and appreciation is the argument Phase 1 established this product should not be leading with.
Implication 2
  • The 2BHK community is the only segment where rental economics are arguable — smaller ticket, proportionally higher yield, and a real tenant pool from AIIMS, SRM, NRI Medical College, KIMS and government offices. If any part of this project is sold on rental logic, it is that one.

The most useful resale observation for phasing

A project launching in 2026 and delivering in phases through 2029–30 would see its first resale cycle coincide with the capital's own Phase I completion. That is a favourable alignment — and an argument for structuring early phases to attract buyers who will still be holding in 2029, rather than short-horizon investors who will be trying to exit into the same window.

§11

What this revises about Phase 1

Three refinements. The first changes the project's risk profile.

11.1 · The premium gap is roughly a third of what Phase 1 estimated

Phase 1 established that ₹500 crore on 25 acres requires a blended ~₹5,900–6,000/sq ft, and benchmarked that against the Mangalagiri locality average of ₹4,062 — a required premium of about 45%.

That was the wrong comparator, and §1 explains why: a locality average blends resale stock, standalone buildings and small units with new gated product. The right comparison is against what comparable product actually achieves.

Corrected finding

The ₹500 crore target requires a blended rate roughly 10–16% above the best comparable product in the same micro-market — and below what premium apartments and villas already achieve elsewhere in the region.

That is an ordinary, achievable positioning task. It is not the 45% stretch Phase 1 described. Phase 1's risk 9.2 — "the premium-pricing gap", rated High impact / High probability — should be downgraded to High impact / Medium probability, conditional on the project delivering a product that genuinely justifies sitting alongside Vertex and above Manjeera.

11.2 · The differentiation argument is now quantified

Phase 1 argued that scale (25 acres vs competitors' 5–7) was the structural differentiator. §5 sharpens it: the real differentiator is not acreage but density — 16–22 units/acre against a corridor apartment norm of 53–113. A three-to-five-fold difference, visible on site, and the concrete thing the price premium is for.

11.3 · Unit count and sales rate revise down

Phase 1 estimated ~490–700 units and 10–12 bookings/month. Applying the observed size distribution (§6) to 8.33 lakh sq ft of saleable area gives ≈400–550 units — the corridor's modal 3BHK and villa are larger than Phase 1 assumed.

Revised sales task
  • 400–550 units over 4–5 years
  • 80–140 units a year
  • 7–11 confirmed bookings a month
Revised marketing envelope
  • Unchanged at ₹20–25 crore (4–5% of GDV)
  • Allowable cost per unit sold rises to ₹3.6–6.2 lakh

§14

What carries into Phase 3

Six usable inputs, five binding constraints.

Established as usable pricing inputs

  1. Corridor segment bands — eight segments, each anchored to named RERA-registered projects with derived rates (§4).
  2. The premium required is ~10–16% over the best local comparable, not 45% over a locality average (§11.1).
  3. Density is the differentiator, and it is quantified — 16–22 units/acre against a corridor norm of 53–113 (§5).
  4. Villas carry the economics — the only product type clearing ₹7,000+/sq ft, appreciating ~3× faster than apartments, with no corridor community exceeding ~70 units (§3).
  5. A four-community structure priced against real comparables reaches ₹506 crore with no community needing to set a record (§12).
  6. Portal base rates understate realisation by 8–15% once floor rise, parking, club, corpus and GST are added (§2).

Established as constraints

  1. Rental yield does not support the premium — 2.2% in Tadepalli, ~3% at best, against 7.15–7.35% loan rates. Do not sell on rental income except, arguably, in Community A (§9).
  2. Absorption is unmeasured and the signals conflict. Model against the slow case (§8).
  3. The ₹2.2–2.8 crore villa band is empty — opportunity or buyer boundary, currently indistinguishable (§12).
  4. The ₹6,300/sq ft apartment ceiling is a Vijayawada city phenomenon. Importing it into the corridor is Community C's central assumption, and it is unproven (§4).
  5. The corridor's best appreciation is east of Vijayawada, not toward the capital (§7). The capital-adjacency price argument is not yet supported by comparative appreciation data.
