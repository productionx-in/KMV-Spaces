# Phase 7 — The ₹500 Crore Business Model

## A transparent funnel, built backwards from revenue, with every number sourced or labelled

**Prepared:** August 2026
**Purpose:** Commercial and marketing-investment model for the Phase 6 community architecture
**Method note:** The brief is explicit — *do not invent benchmarks and present them as facts.* Every input in this document carries one of four classifications, and §3 shows the arithmetic behind every derived figure. Where published sources conflict, §2.4 says so rather than picking the convenient one.

---

## 0. How to read this model

### 0.1 The four classifications

| Tag | Meaning | Confidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **MARKET DATA** | Observed in this catchment during Phases 1–6 — a named project, a published rate, a registry record | Highest |
| **BENCHMARK** | A figure published for the Indian real-estate industry by an identifiable source. **Almost all of these come from marketing agencies, CRM vendors or lead-generation firms — parties with a commercial interest in the numbers.** Treated as directional, never as measurement | Medium, **interested-party flagged** |
| **ASSUMPTION** | A value I have set, with the reasoning shown. Not observed anywhere | Lowest — every one is a test in §8 |
| **CALCULATED** | Derived arithmetically from the above. The formula is shown | As good as its inputs |

### 0.2 The honest position on benchmarks

Phases 1–5 could reach government registries, RERA filings, developer price lists and published pay scales. **Phase 7 needs a different class of data — funnel conversion rates, cost per lead, channel-partner economics — and that data is not published by any authoritative body in India.**

What exists is published by agencies selling lead generation, CRM vendors selling CRM, and consultancies selling consulting. Every one of them has an interest in the numbers looking a particular way. They are used here because they are the only published figures available, and they are labelled **BENCHMARK** with that caveat attached, not upgraded to fact by repetition.

**Two consequences run through this document.** First, the funnel rates are presented as **scenario ranges rather than point estimates**, because a single number would imply a precision the sources do not support. Second, **the model is calibrated against one external anchor** — an industry-normal marketing spend of 4–5% of gross development value — and the base case is required to land inside it. A model that produced a far cheaper answer than the industry norm would be telling me my assumptions were wrong, not that I had found an efficiency.

### 0.3 What this model does not include

Land cost, construction cost, finance cost, approvals, statutory charges, GST, and the in-house sales team's salaries. **This is a revenue and customer-acquisition model, not a project P&L.** Where a margin figure is needed to express marketing ROI, it is stated as an ASSUMPTION and flagged (§6.3).

## 2. Sales velocity, and the inputs behind it

### 2.1 The three windows

**ASSUMPTION** — sales windows of 54, 45 and 36 months. Rationale: Phase 1 §6.4 framed the sell-out as *"4–5 years"*; Phase 6 reduced the unit count from ~600 to 376, which shortens it. A 36-month window is aggressive for a first project of this scale by this developer in this corridor; 54 months is the cautious case.

**CALCULATED** — required run-rate:

| Scenario | Window | Bookings / month | Bookings / year |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Conservative** | 54 months | **7.0** | 84 |
| **Base** | 45 months | **8.4** | 100 |
| **Aggressive** | 36 months | **10.4** | 125 |

**This is a material improvement on Phase 1.** Phase 1 §6.4 estimated *"600 units at 10–12 bookings a month for 4–5 years."* Phase 6's product mix raises average unit size from ~1,389 to 2,210 sq ft and cuts the unit count to 376. **The same revenue now needs 7 to 10 bookings a month instead of 10 to 12** — roughly a 25% easier absorption task at the same GDV.

### 2.2 A caution the model does not capture

**Bookings are not linear.** Real-estate absorption is front-loaded at launch, troughs in the middle, and recovers near completion. A flat 8.4 per month is a planning average, not a forecast. **The practical consequence is cash-flow, not marketing:** a sales plan built on a flat run-rate will look like it is failing in months 12–30 when it is behaving normally. §8 flags this.

### 2.3 Where bookings come from — the input that changes everything

Not every booking is bought with marketing. **ASSUMPTION**, informed by the channel findings in Phase 5 §3:

| | Marketing-sourced | Channel partner | Referral / walk-in | Reasoning |
| --- | --: | --: | --: | --- |
| **A** | 55% | 35% | 10% | Portal-led segment; S5 and S6 both fill enquiry forms. Channel partners active in this band |
| **B** | **70%** | 15% | 15% | Diaspora is reachable only digitally. Phase 5: "digital, entirely." Highest paid-media dependency in the scheme |
| **C** | 45% | 30% | 25% | Phase 5 S1: "referral and personal introduction first… digital is a verification channel, not a discovery channel" |
| **D** | **35%** | 25% | **40%** | The most referral-dependent. Phase 5: *this segment does not buy a project; it buys the other buyers* |
| **Blended** | **53%** | 28% | 19% | |

**CALCULATED — marketing must source 201 of 376 bookings.** The other 175 arrive through channel partners (paid on commission, not on media) and referral (paid for with product and delivery, not with budget). **Sizing the media plan against 376 bookings would over-build it by 87%.**

### 2.4 Where the published sources conflict, and how that is handled

The single most important input in the model — **site-visit-to-booking conversion** — has published figures that disagree by a factor of four.

| Source class | Published figure | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Real-estate marketing-automation blog | **8–20%** typical, **32–51%** for high-performing teams | **BENCHMARK, interested party** |
| Real-estate marketing blog, segment-specific | **3–8%** for the ₹50 lakh – ₹3 crore band | **BENCHMARK, interested party** — and this is the band the project actually sits in |

> **This conflict is not resolvable from published data.** The lower figure is segment-specific to exactly this ticket range and is used as the anchor; the higher figure is treated as the ceiling a mature sales operation might approach.
>
> **The model uses 5.0% / 7.0% / 8.5% at project level** — inside the segment-specific band for conservative and base, and reaching its top for aggressive. **It does not use the 32–51% figure at all.** A model that assumed high-performer conversion for a first-time launch would be assuming away the hardest part of the problem.

## 3. The funnel, built backwards

### 3.1 The chain, and the arithmetic at each step

Working upward from bookings. Each step is **CALCULATED** by dividing by the conversion rate below it.

```
        Revenue  ₹506.7 Cr
             ↑   ÷ revenue per booking
       Bookings  376  (of which marketing must source 201)
             ↑   ÷ site-visit-to-booking %
    Site visits  2,407 – 4,091
             ↑   ÷ qualified-lead-to-visit %
Qualified leads  9,539 – 18,162
             ↑   ÷ lead-to-qualified-lead %
          Leads  54,288 – 129,205
             ↑   × cost per lead
      Lead-gen spend  ₹5.51 – 19.26 Cr
             +   brand & content, sales infrastructure
  Total marketing  ₹15.51 – 34.96 Cr
```

### 3.2 The rate assumptions, stated per stage

**Project-level base rates — ASSUMPTION, anchored where a benchmark exists:**

| Stage | Conservative | Base | Aggressive | Basis |
| --- | --: | --: | --: | --- |
| **Lead → qualified lead** | 16% | 18% | 20% | **ASSUMPTION.** No published Indian real-estate figure was found. Set low because portal and social lead quality is poor; a BENCHMARK exists that developers *without* CRM automation lose 30–50% of qualified leads to follow-up gaps, which is a different metric but points the same way |
| **Qualified lead → site visit** | 25% | 26% | 28% | **ASSUMPTION.** No published figure found. Narrow range because this stage is operational — it depends on response time and follow-up discipline more than on market conditions |
| **Site visit → booking** | 5.0% | 7.0% | 8.5% | **BENCHMARK-anchored** — see §2.4. Inside the 3–8% figure published for the ₹50 lakh–₹3 crore band |
| **Compound lead → booking** | **0.16%** | **0.25%** | **0.37%** | **CALCULATED.** 643 / 393 / 270 leads per marketing-sourced booking |

**Per-community modifiers — ASSUMPTION**, applied to the base rates because the four communities do not behave alike:

| | Lead→QL | QL→visit | Visit→booking | Why |
| --- | --: | --: | --: | --- |
| **A** | ×1.10 | ×1.10 | ×1.15 | Broadest audience, cheapest decision, easiest to get on site |
| **B** | ×0.75 | **×0.70** | ×0.85 | Diaspora leads are noisy, and **the buyer often physically cannot visit** — Phase 5: a proxy relative visits first, and the personal visit is compressed into one India trip |
| **C** | ×1.00 | ×1.05 | ×1.00 | Local, motivated, visits readily |
| **D** | ×0.85 | ×0.95 | **×0.80** | Largest decision in the scheme, longest family consultation, lowest close rate per visit |

### 3.4 The cost lines that are not lead generation

Omitting these is what makes a real-estate marketing model look implausibly cheap.

| Line | Conservative | Base | Aggressive | Classification |
| --- | --: | --: | --: | --- |
| **Brand & content** — brand campaign, print, hoardings, PR, film, collateral, launch events | ₹9.50 Cr | ₹7.50 Cr | ₹6.00 Cr | **ASSUMPTION** |
| **Sales infrastructure** — experience centre, two show homes, CRM and martech stack | ₹6.20 Cr | ₹5.00 Cr | ₹4.00 Cr | **ASSUMPTION** |

**Reasoning for the sales-infrastructure line.** Phase 5 found show-flat quality *"matters disproportionately because it is the only tangible evidence available"* for S6, and that S1 *"will visit two or three times before committing"* with 3–6 family members. A 25-acre scheme selling a ₹2.5 crore villa on the strength of a masterplan that does not exist yet needs a built experience centre and at least one furnished villa. **Treating that as anything other than a marketing cost hides it.**

### 3.5 Channel partner commission — a separate cost class

**BENCHMARK** — published Indian channel-partner commission rates: **1–3% of agreement value** typically; **2–3% for luxury residential and plotted development**; occasionally up to 5% for luxury.

**ASSUMPTION** — 3.0% / 2.5% / 2.0% across the scenarios, applied **only to channel-partner-sourced bookings** (28% of units, §2.3).

> **This is not marketing spend and should never be blended into it.** It is pay-on-performance: it costs nothing until a unit sells, it does not require working capital, and it scales perfectly. Reporting a "marketing cost" that excludes it understates customer acquisition; reporting a blended figure that includes it overstates media efficiency. **This model reports both, separately.**

## 5. The finding the model produced that the strategy did not anticipate

### 5.1 Community B consumes 65% of the lead-generation budget for 22% of the units

**CALCULATED**, and it holds across all three scenarios:

| | Share of units | Share of lead-gen budget | CAC | **CAC as % of ticket** |
| --- | --: | --: | --: | --: |
| **A** | 44.9% | **7.2%** | ₹0.8 L | **1.1%** |
| **B** | 22.3% | **65.4%** | ₹10.9 L | **8.1%** |
| **C** | 16.0% | **7.2%** | ₹2.6 L | **1.4%** |
| **D** | 16.8% | **20.1%** | ₹9.0 L | **3.6%** |

*(Base case. In the conservative case B's CAC reaches **₹21.3 lakh — 15.9% of a ₹1.34 crore ticket.**)*

**Three inputs compound to produce this,** and each is individually reasonable:

1. **B has the highest marketing dependency** — 70% of its bookings must be marketing-sourced, because Phase 5 established the diaspora is reachable *"digitally, entirely."*
2. **B has the highest CPL** — ₹1,600 base, because overseas geo-targeting of a narrow affinity audience is the most expensive media in the plan.
3. **B has the worst qualified-lead-to-visit rate** — a ×0.70 modifier, because Phase 5 found the buyer often *physically cannot visit*: a proxy relative goes first, and the personal visit is compressed into a single India trip.

**Multiply the three and B needs 682 leads per booking against Community A's 219.**

> **This is the most commercially significant output of the model, and it is a genuine surprise.** Phase 5 ranked S3 — the US diaspora — as the **highest-priority segment at 4.20**, on revenue potential, cash-gate clearance and low competition. That ranking was right about the *value* of the segment and silent about the *cost of reaching it*. **The funnel supplies the missing half.**

### 5.2 What follows from it — and what does not

**What does not follow:** abandoning Community B. It is 22% of revenue, it serves the 45% of top-end demand Phase 6 §4.4 built it for, and its CAC is still only 8.1% of ticket in the base case — high, but not disqualifying.

**What does follow — three things:**

**1 · Move B off paid media wherever possible.** Every point of B's marketing-sourced share that shifts to referral or institutional channels removes roughly ₹9 lakh of acquisition cost per unit. Phase 5 named the channels no competitor was using: **APNRTS, headquartered in Guntur; diaspora associations; origin-district rather than current-city targeting; and the inspecting relative in India, who holds effective veto and is almost never the person the media is aimed at.** Marketing to that relative is local media at local CPL against a lead that converts like a resident.

**2 · Fix the visit stage, not the lead stage.** B's binding constraint is qualified-lead-to-visit at 18.2%, not CPL. A **credible remote alternative to the physical visit** — a scheduled live video walkthrough with the sales lead, a proxy-visit protocol for the relative, a recorded specification walk — attacks the ×0.70 modifier directly. Moving B's Q→V from 18.2% to 25% cuts its lead requirement by 27% and its lead-gen spend by roughly ₹1.7 crore in the base case, for the cost of a process.

**3 · Concentrate B's media in the two windows that exist.** Phase 5: this segment buys during the **December–January and summer India visits**, and *"if the site is not ready to close in that window, the sale is lost for a year."* Spending evenly across twelve months on an audience that can only transact in four is the single most avoidable waste in this plan.

### 5.3 The mirror finding — Community C is the most efficient product in the scheme

**CALCULATED.** C delivers **21.6% of revenue for 7.2% of the lead-generation budget**, at a CAC of **₹2.6 lakh on a ₹1.82 crore ticket — 1.4%.**

The reason is structural, not tactical: **55% of C's bookings arrive through channel partners and referral**, which cost commission and reputation rather than media. Phase 5 found S1 *"runs on referral… digital is a verification channel, not a discovery channel."* **The cheapest revenue in this project comes from the segment that cannot be bought with advertising.**

That is a strategic instruction, not an observation. **The referral and channel-partner engine is worth more to this project's economics than any media efficiency available**, and it is built with delivery, seeding and relationships — the things Phase 5 §5.4 said to start early because they compound slowly.

## 6. Unit economics, and one metric that should be handled carefully

### 6.1 The acquisition cost stack — BASE case

| Line | Amount | Per unit | % of revenue |
| --- | --: | --: | --: |
| Lead generation | ₹9.84 Cr | ₹2.62 L | 1.94% |
| Brand & content | ₹7.50 Cr | ₹1.99 L | 1.48% |
| Sales infrastructure | ₹5.00 Cr | ₹1.33 L | 0.99% |
| **Total marketing** | **₹22.34 Cr** | **₹5.94 L** | **4.41%** |
| Channel partner commission | ₹3.34 Cr | ₹0.89 L | 0.66% |
| **Total acquisition** | **₹25.67 Cr** | **₹6.83 L** | **5.07%** |

**Against Phase 5's allowable.** Phase 5 §5.5 computed an allowable marketing cost of **₹5.0–6.3 lakh per unit** at 4–5% of GDV on 397 units. This model, on 376 units, gives **₹5.94 lakh of marketing per unit** — inside that range, and **₹6.83 lakh all-in including commission.** The two phases reconcile.

### 6.2 ROAS, and why it is not a return

**CALCULATED.** ROAS on gross development value: **14.5× / 22.7× / 32.7×.**

> **Those numbers are real and they are close to meaningless as a measure of return.** ROAS here divides **gross sales value** by marketing spend. It takes no account of land, construction, finance or approvals — which in a residential development are the overwhelming majority of the cost.
>
> **A 22.7× ROAS does not mean marketing returns 22.7 rupees for every rupee.** It means every rupee of marketing is associated with 22.7 rupees of *turnover*, most of which is spent building the thing that was sold. Quoting it as a return would be the single most misleading number available in this document, which is why it is stated here with the caveat attached rather than in a headline.

### 6.3 Marketing ROI, computed properly

To express a return, marketing cost has to be set against **margin**, not turnover.

**ASSUMPTION — development gross margin of 25% / 28% / 31% of GDV**, before land finance. This is a placeholder: **Phase 7 has no cost data for this project and does not claim to.** The margin rises across scenarios because a faster sell-out carries less financing and holding cost.

**CALCULATED:**

$$\text{Marketing ROI} = \frac{(\text{Revenue} \times \text{gross margin}) - \text{total acquisition cost}}{\text{total acquisition cost}}$$

| | Conservative | Base | Aggressive |
| --- | --: | --: | --: |
| Gross margin assumption | 25% | 28% | 31% |
| Gross margin, ₹ | ₹126.7 Cr | ₹141.9 Cr | ₹157.1 Cr |
| Total acquisition cost | ₹38.97 Cr | ₹25.67 Cr | ₹18.18 Cr |
| **Marketing ROI** | **2.3×** | **4.5×** | **7.6×** |

**A more robust way to state the same thing — the break-even test**, which does not depend on the margin assumption being right in absolute terms:

| | Conservative | Base | Aggressive |
| --- | --: | --: | --: |
| **Units marketing must source to cover its own cost** | **116** | **68** | **44** |
| Units marketing is modelled to source | 201 | 201 | 201 |
| **Margin of safety** | **1.7×** | **3.0×** | **4.6×** |

> **Even in the conservative case, marketing pays for itself if it sources 116 of the 376 units.** It is modelled to source 201. **The marketing investment is not the fragile part of this business model** — the pricing assumptions in Phase 6 §7 are.

## 7. Sensitivity — what actually moves the answer

**CALCULATED** — base case, one variable moved at a time, all others held.

| Variable moved | Change | Effect on total marketing | Sensitivity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Site-visit-to-booking** | 7.0% → 5.0% | ₹22.34 Cr → **₹31.6 Cr** | **+41%. The most powerful lever in the model** |
| **Marketing-sourced share** | 53% → 43% | ₹22.34 Cr → **₹18.8 Cr** | −16%. Referral and channel partners are the cheapest capacity available |
| **Community B's Q→V rate** | 18.2% → 25% | ₹22.34 Cr → **₹20.6 Cr** | −8% from one community's process fix |
| **Blended CPL** | +25% | ₹22.34 Cr → **₹24.8 Cr** | +11%. Media price matters less than conversion |
| **Sales window** | 45 → 54 months | Fixed costs stretch; lead-gen unchanged | Time costs money through brand and infrastructure, not media |

> **The ranking is the finding.** A 2-point fall in site-visit-to-booking conversion costs **more than a 25% rise in media prices.** The model says the sales gallery, the show homes and the follow-up discipline are worth more than media buying — which is consistent with Phase 5's finding that this catchment's most valuable segments do not discover projects through advertising at all.

## 9. Carry-forward

**MARKET DATA and CALCULATED.**
- **376 units, ₹506.7 crore, average ticket ₹1.35 crore**, blended ₹6,098/sq ft on 8.31 lakh sq ft.
- **123 villas — 33% of units — carry 53% of revenue.** A Community D booking is worth **3.38 Community A bookings**.
- The sales task is **7.0 to 10.4 bookings a month** over 36–54 months — roughly 25% easier than Phase 1's estimate, because the unit count fell from ~600 to 376.
- **Marketing must source only 201 of 376 bookings.** The rest come through channel partners (28%) and referral (19%).

**The recommended plan — BASE case.**
- **₹22.34 crore of marketing, 4.41% of revenue** — lead generation ₹9.84 Cr, brand and content ₹7.50 Cr, sales infrastructure ₹5.00 Cr.
- Plus **₹3.34 crore of channel-partner commission**, pay-on-performance, reported separately.
- **₹25.67 crore total acquisition, 5.07% of revenue, ₹6.83 lakh per unit.**
- Funnel: **78,880 leads → 12,474 qualified → 2,922 site visits → 201 marketing-sourced bookings.** 67 leads a working day, 65 site visits a month.

**The three findings that should change the plan.**
1. **Community B consumes 65% of the lead-generation budget for 22% of units**, at a CAC of 8.1% of ticket rising to 15.9% in the conservative case. Its binding constraint is the **site-visit stage**, not lead cost — and the fix is a remote-visit protocol and the institutional diaspora channels Phase 5 identified, not more media.
2. **Community C is the most efficient product in the scheme** — 21.6% of revenue for 7.2% of lead-gen spend — because 55% of its bookings come through referral and channel partners. **The cheapest revenue here comes from the segment that cannot be bought with advertising.**
3. **Conversion beats media price.** A 2-point fall in site-visit-to-booking costs more than a 25% rise in CPL. Sales infrastructure and follow-up discipline outrank media buying.

**Open.** The eight items in §8 — of which items 1–5 are answerable with 60–90 days of live funnel data and a ₹15–20 lakh pre-launch media test, and **item 8 is not a marketing question at all**: it is Phase 6's pricing risk, and it is larger than everything else in this model combined.
